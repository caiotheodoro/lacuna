\title{Counting the actions no log recorded:\\a stress test of capture-recapture for agent trace completeness}
\author{Caio Theodoro}

\begin{abstract}
Agent audit trails are engineered for integrity, and their completeness is
assumed. When no single log is complete, the number of actions missing from all
of them is a population-size problem, and capture-recapture over logs written by
different infrastructure can estimate it. I stress-test that idea by replaying
recorded episodes of a benign LLM agent, in environments whose kernels keep a
ledger no log reads, under simulated log loss. A pre-registered test in a
back-office environment passed all four of its hypotheses; a pre-registered
transfer to an infra environment failed its coverage hypothesis. Post hoc, the
record linkage the tests registered, matching records by their effect, costs
about five points of coverage against exact matching in back-office and most of
it in infra, and linking logs by an id they share recovers the back-office loss.
Two loss patterns break the estimator under any linkage: loss concentrated on
the actions one log cannot see, which fails silently, and loss in bursts, which
in back-office mostly lands on those same actions. Nothing here tests an agent
that hides on purpose.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Audit regimes that reach AI agents start from a log~\citep{euaiact}, and the
engineering around it answers whether a line was changed after it was
written~\citep{flightrecorder2026,vet2025}. Whether the record is whole is
assumed. Agents have been shown deleting their own trace events when asked, and
tampering unprompted when it raised their reward~\citep{qin2026tamper}; a line
never written leaves no trace in a hash chain.

The count of actions no log shows needs estimating only when no single record is
complete. A gateway that numbered every action before anything was dropped would
give it exactly. Where actions bypass the gateway, logs belong to parties that do
not share their numbering, or sampling comes first, epidemiology's method
applies: overlapping several incomplete registers estimates what all of them
missed~\citep{fienberg1972,bird2018mse}. I found no work applying it to agent
trace completeness; the nearest, over LLM judges counting label errors, produced
valid intervals too wide to beat a raw count~\citep{kansal2026labels}.

The contribution is a stress test on recorded episodes, not a deployed auditor.
With loss that is independent by construction and exact matching, the estimator
is nominal, as it should be. The interest is in what breaks it:
\begin{itemize}
\item the record linkage an auditor can compute (\S\ref{sec:linkage});
\item loss that depends on which action it is, and loss in bursts
(\S\ref{sec:loss}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

An \emph{action} is a tool call that changes environment state or sends data
out. The environment kernel appends one ledger entry per action, so the true
count $N$ is a program, and no witness reads it. Three witnesses observe actions:
W1, the harness's trace of the agent's tool calls; W2, a gateway proxy log; W3,
an end-of-shift diff of the environment's state plus its outbound mail sink.

\paragraph{Estimator and assumption.} \texttt{ll\_bic} fits Poisson log-linear
models to the seven observed cells of the three-log capture
table~\citep{fienberg1972}, selects pairwise interactions by BIC and reports a
95\% profile-likelihood interval for $N$. It assumes there is no three-way
interaction: the logs may depend on each other in pairs, but what all three miss
together is predicted by how they overlap. An agent that evades all three at once
violates exactly that, and in a parametric simulator of that case every point
estimator covers 0.218 or less.

\paragraph{Linkage.} Matching records across logs \emph{exactly} uses the ledger
id, which no auditor has. \emph{Effect-signature} matching pairs records on
operation, resource and, for some operations, value, in order within a signature.

\paragraph{Replays.} A live agent (\texttt{deepseek-v4.1-flash}, benign) worked
two environments: a back-office queue of refunds, cancellations, customer updates
and email, and an infra/ops queue whose end-state diff misses transient work.
Before any loss, the trace and the gateway each held every action. Loss is
therefore simulated afterwards on the recorded episodes. Each \emph{replicate}
resamples the episodes with replacement, drops log lines by the loss model, and
re-estimates $N$; \emph{coverage} is the share of replicates whose interval
contains the true $N$, and \emph{gap detection} the share whose lower end exceeds
the number of distinct actions any log saw. In the held-out configuration
\texttt{fin\_mid}, each of the trace and the gateway loses half its lines at
random, and the env diff sees only financial operations. A typical replicate
there hides 63.15 of 828 actions from every log, and \texttt{ll\_bic}'s interval
is 42.0 wide. The pre-registered bar was 0.90 coverage for a 95\% interval.

\section{The pre-registered tests}
\label{sec:prereg}

Both tests were committed to the repository before their paid runs, on loss
configurations held out of the estimator choice, with effect-signature matching
as the headline. The back-office run deviated twice from its protocol: a crash
forced a rerun on the same seeds, and three episodes lost to provider errors were
later restored on a changed client.

The back-office test passed all four hypotheses on the registered analysis, with
coverage 0.940. Re-estimated over 30 fresh loss seeds on the same 21 episodes,
signature-linkage coverage is 0.893 and exact-matching coverage 0.948
(Table~\ref{tab:precision}); the quoted errors cover loss draws only, not which
episodes were recorded, so the first is not distinguishable from the bar. The
stable finding is the gap between the linkages. The infra test failed its
coverage hypothesis: 0.300 at the registered seed, 0.401 over 20 seeds, against
0.946 with exact matching (Table~\ref{tab:transfer}). Its other three hypotheses
passed but say little: signature matching there claims a gap even when nothing is
hidden, which empties the two gap-detection tests, and the dependence test passes
because the interval doubles around an unchanged bias.

Two caveats bound the infra result. The paid run reproduced a free scripted one:
1035 of the agent's 1036 actions are in the scripted plans, so it tests the
environment and the linkage, not the agent. And its verdict depends on how many
actions collide, which the ticket mix sets: in scripted runs, signature coverage
is 0.860 at 10 tickets per episode, 0.155 at 60, and 0.905 when 30 tickets are
spread over 30 services instead of 10.

\section{What the registered linkage costs}
\label{sec:linkage}

In back-office, 0.223 of actions share an effect signature with another action in
the same episode, almost all of them emails to the same customer. In-order pairing
lines up records of different actions as one action seen by both the trace and
the gateway. BIC reads that as a trace--gateway dependence and fits it in 0.358 of
replicates, against 0.010 under exact matching; the independence model, which
cannot fit it, covers 0.948. In infra, identical repeated actions merge away and
the env diff is mis-attached. The same mechanism produces false gaps: when the
two logs lose mutually exclusive actions, so that nothing is hidden, signature
matching claims a gap in up to every replicate (Table~\ref{tab:linkage}).

The signature left out content every log carries, and the harness writes the
provider's tool-call id into both the trace and the gateway. Linking those two
logs by that id restores 0.946 in back-office under the registered view, where the
env diff sees every email and is easy to attach, and removes every false gap
measured, which is close to true by construction once the two logs are linked
exactly. With the full view it is 0.924, and in infra 0.859: the env diff carries
no id, and attaching its rows to identical repeated actions needs a per-row id or
timestamp. A real gateway assigns its own request ids, so this needs the harness,
or the infrastructure, to stamp one id on every log; an agent that writes its own
trace could forge it, and that was not tested.

\section{Loss the estimator does not expect}
\label{sec:loss}

\paragraph{Loss that depends on the action.} Raising both logs' drop rate on the
operations the env diff cannot see breaks \texttt{ll\_bic} silently. Under exact
matching it covers 0.647 at a tenth more loss and 0.187 at a fifth more, on
intervals of ordinary width that still assert the gap (Table~\ref{tab:dose}).
Stratifying on the operation covers where it answers, refusing more often as the
strata thin; this is its best case, since the loss is keyed on the stratifying
field.

\paragraph{Loss in bursts.} Dropping blocks of 12 consecutive actions per log,
each with probability one half and under exact matching, takes \texttt{ll\_bic}
to 0.588 in back-office and 0.772 in infra (Table~\ref{tab:controls}). In
back-office this is mostly not burstiness. The agent works one kind of ticket at a
time, so the actions the financial view cannot see sit in one stretch of each
episode, and a lost block that lands there hides actions no witness then sees.
Keeping the order but permuting which actions the diff sees restores 0.875 to
0.940. On the long-episode pools, where that stretch is longest, the mean error
is $+29.6$ and $+58.8$, carried by a minority of replicates in which BIC selects a
spurious interaction. In infra the diff's blind spots are spread through the
episode, the view cannot be permuted, and the failure is mostly variance: the bias
is $+1.7$.

A quasi-likelihood correction~\citep{wedderburn1974quasi,mccullagh1989glm}, with
the dispersion taken from variation between episodes, trades width for coverage.
Applied to the interval only, it keeps BIC's point and covers 0.966 in back-office
and 0.994 in infra at 2.2 to 3.4 times the width, but on the long-episode pools it
covers 0.877 and 0.846 by widening around a biased point, and detects the gap in
only 0.73 and 0.41 of replicates. Applied at model selection as well, it removes
most of the bias: on one set of \texttt{long-60} draws it covers 0.934 at width
116.1, against 351.6 for the interval-only version. But when session quality varies and hits all three logs at
once it drops the interactions that were absorbing that dependence and collapses
to 0.009. Neither is a default; both were found after the runs.

\section{What this does not show}
\label{sec:limits}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{No adversary and no real log loss.} Every agent was benign, the
loss was simulated on complete logs, and the replays use 21 to 24 episodes of one
model family. Coverage errors cover loss draws, not episode sampling.
\item \textbf{The environments and the bar were mine.} The infra run is
scripted-equivalent and its verdict depends on a collision density I set.
\item \textbf{The fixes are post hoc}, found on the same episodes that showed the
problems.
\item \textbf{Cheaper checks were not compared.} Seeded canary actions and
sequence-number gaps measure log loss directly and were not run head to head; in
back-office every action passes the gateway, so a gateway that numbered its action
entries first would make this method unnecessary there.
\end{itemize}

\section{Takeaways for an auditor}

Capture-recapture over independent logs can assert that a record is incomplete
and, under the right conditions, say by how much. Those conditions are narrow. The
logs must be linked by an id they share, not by what the actions did. Loss must
not concentrate, by kind or by time, on the actions one log cannot see. And the
interval should be reported beside a dispersion-corrected one, with the gap
detection of each. Where those fail, the method's honest output is that actions
are missing, not how many.

\section*{Reproducing this}

The repository holds both environments, the estimators, every recorded episode,
a decision log recording each prediction made before a run and each result that
contradicted one, and every artifact a number here comes from, with a technical
report giving the full results; it will be released with the capture histories
as a dataset. All live runs with a receipt cost \$5.92.
