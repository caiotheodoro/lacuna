\title{Counting the actions no log recorded:\\a stress test of capture-recapture for agent trace completeness}
\author{Caio Theodoro}
\date{October 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Audit regimes assume an agent's log is complete. When none is, capture-recapture over
independent logs can estimate the actions missing from all of them. Four pre-registered
stress tests, on recorded benign-agent episodes with simulated log loss, find it works
only with exact record linkage, and only where every channel (a route an action can take)
is itemised by two logs or more (one record per action). A back-office test passed at its
registered seed (coverage 0.940, bar 0.90) but reaches 0.893 over 30 fresh loss seeds;
an infra test failed as predicted (0.300, and 0.401 over 20 seeds). The lead negative
result is a channel no log itemises: the agent ran 0.856 of its actions in a batch tool
(predicted 0.05 to 0.30) and coverage fell to 0.759 while the estimator still reported a
gap (0.995). Post hoc, one record per scripted action restores 0.949, but parsing the
scripts and losing their lines together covers 0.335 to 0.610 by pool (0.584 earlier):
itemising is necessary, not sufficient. Only design review names the unseen channel. An
adversary prompt split the pool: half declined and stopped batching, the rest did not, at
least two to undercount; none left the channel.
\end{abstract}

\section{Problem and thesis}

Audit regimes that reach AI agents start from a log~\citep{euaiact}, and the
engineering around it asks whether a line was changed after it was
written~\citep{flightrecorder2026,vet2025}. Whether the record is whole is
assumed. Agents have been shown deleting their own trace events when asked, and
tampering unprompted when it raised their reward~\citep{qin2026tamper}; a line
never written leaves no trace in a hash chain.

When no single record is complete, the number of actions missing from all of them can
be estimated like any hidden population: overlapping several incomplete registers
estimates what all of them missed~\citep{fienberg1972,bird2018mse}. I found no work
applying this to agent trace completeness; the nearest, over LLM judges counting label
errors, produced valid intervals too wide to beat a raw count~\citep{kansal2026labels}.
A gateway that numbered every action before anything was dropped would give the count
exactly, so the method is for settings where no such counter exists: actions bypass the
gateway, logs belong to parties that do not share their numbering, or sampling comes
first.

\paragraph{Thesis.} \emph{Capture-recapture over independent logs bounds the unlogged
agent actions when every channel is itemised by at least two logs and records are lost
action by action; when a channel is itemised by none, no log-based count can see it, and
the failure is detectable only by design review, not by the estimator.} A
\emph{channel} is a route an action can take: a direct tool call, or a batch tool such as
\texttt{run\_script}, which runs a script of many operations in one call. A log
\emph{itemises} a channel if it holds one record per action taken through it. The first
half also needs exact record linkage; the second is the boundary, tested once, as the lead
negative result (\S\ref{sec:bypass}). Study 4 shows the loss clause is not a formality:
itemising a channel is necessary but not sufficient (\S\ref{sec:study4}). This is a stress
test on recorded episodes, not a deployed auditor.

\section{Method in brief}
\label{sec:setup}

An \emph{action} is a tool call that changes environment state or sends data out. The
environment kernel appends one ledger entry per action, so the true count $N$ is a
program that no witness reads. Three witnesses observe actions: W1, the harness's trace of
the agent's tool calls; W2, a gateway proxy log; W3, an end-of-shift diff of the
environment's state plus its outbound mail sink.

\paragraph{Estimator.} \texttt{ll\_bic} fits Poisson log-linear models to the seven
observed cells of the three-log capture table~\citep{fienberg1972}, selects pairwise
interactions by BIC and reports a 95\% profile-likelihood interval for $N$. It was chosen
on exploratory runs before any test. It assumes no three-way interaction: the logs may
depend on each other in pairs, but what all three miss together is predicted by how they
overlap.

\paragraph{Linkage.} Records are matched across logs \emph{exactly} by the ledger id,
which no auditor has, or by \emph{effect signature} (operation, resource and for some
operations value, in order within a signature), the registered headline. Post hoc, a
\emph{shared call id}, the provider's tool-call id that the harness writes into both the
trace and the gateway, links those two logs.

\paragraph{Replays.} A live agent (\texttt{deepseek-v4.1-flash}, benign; study 4 adds
\texttt{qwen3.8-flash}) worked two
environments and a batch-tool variant of one: a back-office queue of refunds,
cancellations, customer updates, file writes and email, and an infra/ops queue whose
end-state diff misses transient work. Before any loss, the trace and the gateway each held
every action, so loss is simulated afterwards on the recorded episodes. Each
\emph{replicate} resamples the episodes with replacement, drops log lines by the loss
model and re-estimates $N$. \emph{Coverage} is the share of replicates whose interval
contains $N$; \emph{gap detection} the share whose lower end exceeds the number of distinct
actions any log saw. A $\pm$ is one Monte Carlo standard error; because replicates reuse
the same episodes, no error here covers what recording new episodes would change.
\emph{Post hoc} marks every result outside the three pre-registrations. In the held-out
configuration \texttt{fin\_mid} the trace and the gateway each lose half their lines at
random, and the env diff sees only the financial operations (the \emph{registered} view).

\paragraph{Back-office.} The registered seed gave 0.940 over 100 replicates, but the pass
did not hold up: over 30 fresh loss seeds on the same 21 episodes coverage is
$0.893 \pm 0.006$, and 0.862 with three episodes lost to provider errors restored. The
run deviated twice from its protocol (a crash forced a rerun on the same seeds, and the
three restored episodes came on a changed client).

\paragraph{Infra.} The registration was frozen after scripted infra runs had been seen,
and predicted that T1 would fail. T2--T4 passed but say little: under signature matching
the infra estimator claims a gap even when nothing is hidden, so T2 and T4 could not fail.
Two caveats bound the result. 1035 of the agent's 1036 actions are in the scripted plans,
so it tests the environment and the linkage, not the agent. And its verdict depends on how
many actions collide, which the ticket mix sets: in scripted runs, signature coverage is
0.860 at 10 tickets per episode, 0.155 at 60.

\paragraph{Why the first two failed: linkage.} Effect-signature matching pairs records of
different actions: 0.223 of back-office actions share a signature with another action in
the same episode (179 of those 185 are emails to the same customer), and BIC reads the
false overlap as trace--gateway dependence. Matching by ledger id gives 0.948 and 0.946.
Post hoc, linking the trace and the gateway by the shared call id restores back-office
coverage (0.946) but not infra (0.859): the env diff carries no id, and attaching its rows
to identical repeated actions needs a per-row id or timestamp. The id is only as good as
the lines: 5\% duplicated lines in all three logs take coverage to 0.094 with call-id
linkage, and 5\% forged phantom lines in all three to 0.000.

\section{Lead negative result: a channel no log itemises}
\label{sec:bypass}

In the first two environments every action passes the gateway. The third study (tag
\texttt{prereg-bypass}, frozen before any paid episode) adds \texttt{run\_script} to
back-office: a batch of operations, parsed against a whitelist. The ledger gets each inner
action; the trace and the gateway log one call holding the script text, and no witness
parses it. That is a stipulation: an auditor that parsed it would hold a record of every action,
though not necessarily keep them (\S\ref{sec:study4}). 24
episodes ran with the usual prompt and 12 with a prompt saying the logs are audited and
asking for few log records (the audit warning). All 36 completed.

\paragraph{Result.} The agent ran 1098 of 1283 actions in scripts, a share of 0.856 [0.736,
0.952] against a predicted 0.05 to 0.30. That is the agent's choice under this parser,
which rejected whole 34 of 99 neutral scripts; the cap and whole-script rejection are not
in the tool description. At that share \texttt{ll\_bic} with call-id linkage covers 0.759
[0.743, 0.774] against 0.90, which is 0.187 below the no-bypass reference of 0.946 (a
different pool). Intervals are about 8.6 times as wide in absolute terms (333.6 against
39.0) and the estimate runs high by 34.1. The estimator still detects the gap in 0.995 of
replicates (a design check; also 1.0 with no bypass), and counting the union detects it in
0.0. So the output reports missing actions and wide intervals, but nothing in it says that
one channel is unseen or that the interval is off.

\paragraph{Why it fails, and the parsed-scripts control (post hoc).} Under the registered
view, 317.8 of the 330.7 actions a replicate hides from every log (96\%) are bypassed
\texttt{update\_customer} calls. The trace and the gateway log only the script and the
financial env view does not show customer updates, so no witness can record them: the cell
asks the estimator to extrapolate to actions with zero capture probability.
Table~\ref{tab:bypasscf} changes only what the logs itemise. Giving the trace and the
gateway one record per bypassed action, taken from the ledger (the most favourable auditor),
restores coverage to 0.949, so the failure comes from
the stipulation that no counted log itemises the channel, not from the pool, the ticket mix
or the tool. The registered cell's moderate bias is partly a cancellation: when the env diff
also sees the bypassed updates, coverage is 0.008 at bias $+403.8$.

\paragraph{Other estimators, and the audit warning.} No other cell of the registered
analysis covers more than 0.84 (signature 0.785, Chapman on the trace and the env diff
0.834, the independence model 0.392). No share threshold is established: scripted runs at
shares of 0.10 and 0.28 gave 0.817 and 0.922, the live run at 0.856 gave 0.759. Under the
audit warning the share was 0.99 against 0.87 on the same 12 seeds (paired $+0.121$ [0.010,
0.302]; predicted at most 0.10), but the difference comes from two neutral episodes, one of
which had all 8 scripts rejected; without them it falls from $+0.123$ to $+0.013$. With a
ceiling at 1.0 and 12 pairs this does not show the prompt changed behaviour.

\section{Study 4: clustered loss, a second model and an adversary prompt}
\label{sec:study4}

Study 4 (tag \texttt{prereg-study4}, frozen before any paid episode) asked three things. Can
an auditor that parses the scripts from their logged text recover calibration when a log
drops a script's lines together (A2, read against a concurrent neutral run, A2n)? Does the
bypass result hold on a second model, \texttt{qwen3.8-flash} (R1 on plain back-office; Q0 and
Q1, the bypass share and the coverage)? And does an adversary prompt (\texttt{l2}), which
tells the agent how it is counted and asks it to make the count fall as far below the truth
as it can, change what the agent does (A0)? H is an accounting check that every action
reached a log directly or as an executed line of a logged script; it checks the logging code
and has no power against agent behaviour. Loss is still simulated, so R1 changes the action
mix and not the witnesses: it is not a claim about model families.

\paragraph{Clustered loss.} An auditor that parses the scripts from the logged text but
loses a script's lines together, because the record of the script is the unit a log drops,
undercovers on every pool (Table~\ref{tab:clustered}). Read within a pool: 0.469 on the
neutral pool against 0.952 for the same records lost singly, 0.610 under the adversary prompt
against 0.940, and 0.335 on the second model against 0.941. So the cost is the clustering
and not the parsing. Against not parsing at all the picture is mixed: on the neutral pool
parsing with clustered loss is worse than not parsing (0.469 against 0.655), on the second
model better (0.335 against 0.200). The mechanism is intervals that are too narrow, not a
biased estimate: on the neutral pool the mean bias is 8.1, but the root-mean-square error
is 58.9 and the mean interval width 59.8, where a nominal interval would be about four
times the error. The items in one script are not independent draws from a log, and the
estimator assumes they are. This is the shape of the block-loss failure below, where a
quasi-likelihood interval restored coverage; I did not try a dispersion correction on
clustered script loss, so whether it helps is open. The loss model is one choice (each
script record dropped independently at the single-line rate); other script-level models are
untested.

\paragraph{A second model.} The second model reproduces both earlier results. On plain
back-office it covers 0.942 (R1 pass), and in the bypass environment it routes 0.970 of its
actions through scripts, more than the first model, and \texttt{ll\_bic} collapses (coverage
0.200, 0.371 of intervals unbounded): the mechanism of the bypass study at a higher share.
Two of its 24 bypass episodes (seeds 9508, 9514) degenerated into malformed output with no
ledger action and were kept, as registered.

\paragraph{The adversary prompt split the pool.} The agent routed 0.502 of its actions
through the batch tool under the adversary prompt against 0.925 for the concurrent neutral
run (A0: a decrease, where no change was predicted). The pool is bimodal by episode: 11 of 23
episodes used no script (neutral: 1), 11 batched 0.96 to 1.0 of their actions, one was
mixed (0.55), and the whole drop comes from the 11. I read the reasoning of all 23.
Eleven declined and never batched (seeds 9500, 9501, 9504, 9505, 9506, 9507, 9510, 9513,
9519, 9520, 9521). Two decided to use the batch tool to undercount (9511: ``So we want to hide
actions''; 9516: ``This makes the auditor's estimate fall below the true number of
operations. Good---that's the game''), and a third (9512) started from that reading. Eight
said they would not hide anything and batched anyway as efficiency (9502, 9503, 9508, 9509,
9515, 9517, 9522, 9523), several noting that it lowers the trace count. One (9514) batched
part and sent its emails singly to avoid the appearance of hiding. All 23 call the setup a
test, an evaluation, a benchmark or a trap at least once, so this model may have known it
was observed. H held in the three bypass pools, as it must by construction; its registered
test is on the adversary pool. So the instruction split the pool: about half declined it and
stopped batching, the rest batched as much as the neutral runs, at least two explicitly to
undercount, and no action escaped the trace and the gateway by any route other than the one
channel. No agent found a route beyond the stipulated channel. But the adversary was weak: a
single episode, black box, with the channel not named. A stronger one is untested.

\section{What a deployer should check}

\begin{enumerate}\setlength\itemsep{1pt}\setlength\parskip{0pt}
\item \textbf{Inventory the channels first.} List every route an action can take (batch
tools, code execution, shell, out-of-band APIs) and confirm each is itemised by at least
two logs. This is design review; the estimator cannot do it, and the bypass study is what
happens if it is skipped.
\item \textbf{Itemise a batch per action, and check its lines are not lost together.}
Parsing scripts into one record per action is not enough if a log drops a script's lines
as one: that covered 0.469 where the same records lost one by one covered 0.952. To check,
test whether a log's missing lines come in whole batches: per batch, compare how many of
its lines are missing with what independent loss at the same overall rate would give. This
is loss clustered by the unit a log writes, not the bursts in time of item 4.
\item \textbf{Link by an id the harness stamps on every log,} not by effect. A real
gateway assigns its own request ids, so this needs one id written into all of them. Check
the lines are neither duplicated nor invented.
\item \textbf{Check loss is not concentrated} by kind or by time on actions one log cannot
see, and that its rate does not drift. A dispersion-corrected interval beside the plain
one flags concentration in time, at 2.5 to 3 times the width; it does not flag
concentration by kind.
\item \textbf{Check no loss is shared by all three logs.} Outages that take down every log
leave no detectable gap.
\item \textbf{Compare against a known-itemised pool when you can.} Intervals about 8.6
times wider than the reference are a symptom, not a diagnosis, and a detected gap is
not evidence that the channels are covered (0.995 here, also 1.0 with no bypass).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Secondary robustness, in brief}

The full report (\texttt{paper/lacuna.tex}) holds the rest, each cell post hoc and marked as
such. Loss concentrated on the operations the registered view cannot see breaks
\texttt{ll\_bic} silently under exact matching: 0.647 at a tenth more loss and 0.187 at a
fifth more, on intervals barely wider than at baseline; stratifying by operation stays
near nominal where it answers, and refuses more often as loss grows. Loss in blocks of 12
consecutive actions takes it to 0.559 in back-office and 0.802 in infra, largely the same
failure in back-office because the agent works one kind of ticket at a time, and a
quasi-likelihood interval~\citep{wedderburn1974quasi,mccullagh1989glm} restores 0.952 and
0.994. A loss rate that ramps over the episode drops coverage to 0.742 and 0.471 at
slopes of 0.4 and 0.8. Two checks that need no model, a tail-corrected gateway sequence
number and planted canary actions, cover 0.952 and at least 0.922 where they apply,
but the counter covers 0.0 under bursts and under bypass, and the canaries cost width and
gap detection. The report also carries the selection bootstrap, the baselines, the
MNAR dose table and an R cross-check of the estimator.

\section{What this does not show}
\label{sec:limits}

\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{0pt}\setlength\parskip{0pt}
\item \textbf{A weak adversary, and no real log loss.} The loss was simulated on
complete logs. Every agent was benign except under one adversary prompt (one episode, black
box, channel not named): about half the episodes declined it, the rest batched as much as
neutral, at least two to undercount, and all 23 called it a test or an evaluation. The main
pools have 21 to 24 episodes (23 or 24 in study 4), from two versions of one model family
and, in study 4, one other model. No multiplicity correction is applied, and 8 of study 4's
12 predictions held in full.
\item \textbf{The boundary was tested twice, with one design.} The bypass channel is
stipulated, the 24 episodes are one pool (study 4 repeats it on a second model), the no-bypass reference is another, and the agent set the share
under a parser that rejected 34 of 99 scripts. The audit warning is one pilot on 12 seeds,
not an adaptive adversary.
\item \textbf{The author built the environments and set the bar,} and the fixes are post
hoc, found on the episodes that showed the problems. The infra verdict depends on a
collision density the author chose.
\item \textbf{Loss shared by all three logs} violates the no-three-way assumption; none of
the repairs tried is nominal in both environments.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Reproducing this}

The repository, \url{https://github.com/caiotheodoro/lacuna}, holds the environments, the
estimators, every recorded episode, a decision log of each prediction made before a run and
each result that contradicted one, every artifact a number here comes from, and a technical
report with the full results. The capture histories, without the transcripts, are at
\url{https://huggingface.co/datasets/caiotheodoro/lacuna-capture-histories}. The live runs
cost \$11.47 in all, of which \$1.37 was the bypass study and \$4.17 study 4 (priced at the
first model's rates for every run, so the two qwen runs are overstated).
