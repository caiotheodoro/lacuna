\title{Counting the actions no log recorded:\\capture-recapture for agent trace completeness}
\author{Caio Theodoro}
\date{October 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Agent audit trails are engineered for integrity: hash chains, append-only
servers, attested execution. Their completeness is assumed. Agents have been
shown to delete and spoof their own trace events, and nothing in the stack says
how many consequential actions are missing from the record. When no single log
sees every action, that count is a population-size problem. Three logs written
by different infrastructure --- the agent's own trace, a gateway proxy log and an
end-of-shift environment diff --- each see part of what an agent did, and
capture-recapture over their overlaps estimates the actions none of them saw.

On a back-office environment whose kernel keeps a ledger no witness reads, I
pre-registered a test on loss configurations held out of the estimator choice.
With 63.15 of 828 actions per replicate invisible to every log, a BIC-selected
log-linear estimator asserted that the logs were incomplete in every replicate.
Its coverage passed the pre-registered 0.90 bar at 0.940 on the registered loss
seed. Averaged over four loss seeds it is 0.915, about one Monte Carlo error
above the bar, and with the three episodes the run lost restored it is 0.877, a
failure by the rule fixed before that analysis. When the gateway and the trace
fail together, the independence model collapses to 0.000 coverage while the
selected model holds.

Most of what this paper establishes is where the method fails. An LLM auditor
reading the agent's transcript is not a witness: its recall was 1.0000 with the
call log in view and 0.0000 without it. When log loss concentrates on the
actions no witness sees, the method still detects incompleteness but cannot
measure it; conditioning on an observed covariate turns its wrong answers into
refusals, and a covariate it cannot observe defeats it. And in the one
configuration tested where two logs specialise, the env diff is partial and
records are matched by signature, it reports a gap that is not there.
\end{abstract}

\section{Integrity is engineered; completeness is assumed}

Every audit regime that reaches an AI agent starts from a log. The record-keeping
article of the EU AI Act~\citep{euaiact}, the event logging in ISO/IEC 42001 and
the incident reports that California's SB~53 now requires all assume a record of
what the system did. The engineering around that record has converged on one
property. Logs are hash-chained, committed to Merkle trees, written to
append-only servers, notarised inside trusted execution
environments~\citep{flightrecorder2026,vet2025}. Every one of these answers
whether a line was changed after it was written.

None of them answers whether the record is whole. A deleted line in a hash chain
is detectable. A line that was never written is not. Qin et al.\ showed agents
deleting their own trace events, inserting fake ones and spoofing tool calls when
asked, and doing it unprompted when it raised their reward~\citep{qin2026tamper}.
Their proposed fix is an independent interception server with an append-only
log. It is not evaluated, and they note its limit themselves: it would prove
that model outputs were not altered afterwards, not that tool calls ran as
reported.

The field measures trace integrity and assumes trace completeness. The quantity
an auditor needs is the second one: how many consequential actions the agent took
that no log shows.

That count only needs estimating when no single record is complete. If every
action passes one channel, and that channel numbers its entries before anything is
sampled or dropped, the gaps in its numbering give the missing count exactly and
nothing in this paper is needed. The condition fails in three common ways. An
action can reach the world without passing the numbered channel: a code-execution
side channel, a direct database write. The logs can belong to different parties
that do not expose their numbering: a vendor's trace, a customer's gateway, a
SaaS audit log. Or sampling can happen before numbering, so the numbered stream is
already thinned. In the back-office environment below every action does pass the
gateway, so a gateway that numbered its entries first would recover $N$ exactly.
The live experiments there calibrate the estimator where its answer can be
checked. They do not show it is needed there.

The method for that count is old. Epidemiologists estimate the casualties no
register recorded by overlapping several incomplete
registers~\citep{fienberg1972,bird2018mse}, and software teams once estimated the
defects no inspector found the same way~\citep{eick1992inspection,petersson2004inspection}.
If three logs each miss some of an agent's actions, and miss them for different
reasons, the actions two logs share and the third lacks say how much all three
lacked. I found no published work that applies multiple-systems estimation to
agent trace completeness. The nearest attempt, capture-recapture over LLM judges
to count label errors, produced intervals that were valid and too wide to beat a
raw count~\citep{kansal2026labels}. That failure mode --- width, not coverage ---
runs through everything below.

I built a back-office environment whose kernel keeps a ledger no witness can
read, ran a live LLM agent through it, and asked three questions in order. First,
whether the obvious extra witness, an LLM reading the agent's transcript, is a
witness at all (\S\ref{sec:auditor}). It is not. Second, whether
capture-recapture recovers the hidden count when logs lose lines
(\S\ref{sec:live}--\S\ref{sec:confirm}). The pre-registered test passed on its
registered analysis, by a margin that more loss seeds shrink to about one Monte
Carlo error, and fails once the episodes the run lost are restored.
Third, where it stops working (\S\ref{sec:limit}): when the loss
concentrates on the actions no witness sees.

\section{The estimand, and the floor every method has to beat}
\label{sec:estimand}

An \emph{action} is a tool call that changes environment state or sends data out.
Reads are excluded. The environment kernel appends one ledger entry per action,
so $N$, the number of actions in an episode, is a program and not a label. No
witness reads the ledger.

Three witnesses observe actions, each through infrastructure the others do not
share:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
witness & source & blind spot \\
\midrule
W1 trace & the harness appends each tool call to a log file & sampling, export loss \\
W2 gateway & a proxy logs each tool call & sampling \\
W3 env & end-of-shift state diff plus a network sink & repeats collapse, reverts vanish \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Each action gets a capture history: which witnesses recorded it. Over three
witnesses that is a table of $2^3$ cells, and the cell for ``recorded by none''
is never observed. Estimating that cell is the whole problem.

Records are matched across witnesses in two ways. \emph{Oracle} linkage uses the
ledger id and is the upper bound on what matching can achieve.
\emph{Effect-signature} linkage matches on operation, resource and value, which
is what an auditor without ground truth can compute. Identifiers the agent wrote
are never used, because an agent that hides actions can forge them.

Three baselines are reported in every table. \texttt{trust\_trace} believes the
agent's own log. \texttt{union\_count} counts every distinct action any witness
saw; it is never above $N$, so once anything is hidden it never covers.
\texttt{union\_bound} reads that count as the interval $[n, \infty)$. It covers
every time and says nothing, which makes it the floor in the precise sense that a
method earns its place only by giving a finite upper end without losing coverage.

Coverage alone cannot separate a useful interval from a wide one, so every
estimator also reports \textbf{gap detection}: the share of replicates whose lower
end exceeds the union of all witnesses. A method that detects the gap is asserting
``these logs are missing actions, at 95\%''. \texttt{union\_bound} has gap
detection 0 by construction. Gap detection has false positives of its own, and
\S\ref{sec:limit} measures when.

\section{The witness that copies the trace}
\label{sec:auditor}

The first fourth witness anyone would add is an auditor: an LLM that reads the
agent's transcript and lists the actions it took. Arslan found that LLM
accountability layers relay the conclusions of the agents they audit instead of
checking them, finding a fault's true origin 4.1\% of the time when no agent
proposed it~\citep{arslan2026audit}. That predicts an auditor whose misses
coincide with the trace's. I measured something more extreme.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\input{tables/auditor}
\caption{The LLM auditor against the ledger. With the full transcript it is
perfect; with the agent's narration alone it reports nothing.}
\label{tab:auditor}
\end{table}

Reading the full transcript, the auditor recalled every action in 50 episodes
(Table~\ref{tab:auditor}), with no false positives, up to the longest sessions the
agent would sustain. I had predicted, in writing, that recall would fall with
session length. It did not at any length reached. The agent, and not the queue, set the
longest length: actions per ticket fell from 1.31 at 30 tickets to 0.61 at 120,
because it stopped working long queues to the end.

Reading only what the agent said, the auditor recalled nothing in 10 episodes. It
was working --- it returned a parseable empty list every time --- and the agent
does narrate. Its closing message in one episode reads ``All 31 queue items are
handled: O26 and O17 cancelled (with emails), nine refunds issued \ldots\ all
address/email updates applied with duplicates ignored.'' That account is
aggregate. An audit record needs the customer, the field and the amount, and
``nine refunds'' is a count that cannot be overlapped with anything.

The auditor was the same model as the agent. In hindsight the result is close
to obvious. It is reported because an LLM auditor is the first independent
witness most designs reach for. I tried two settings, and neither sits in
between: the only record of what the agent did is its call log, and the call
log is W1. In both settings the auditor was either a copy of the trace or empty, and a witness with capture probability 1 pins $N$ to the
observed count. The study below uses three witnesses. The auditor stays in the
repository as a measured dead end.

\section{Estimators, and where each one is identified}
\label{sec:estimators}

\textbf{Log-linear multiple-systems estimation}~\citep{fienberg1972} fits the
seven observed cells with a Poisson model: a main effect per witness plus chosen
pairwise interactions. The never-seen cell is the intercept. An interaction is a
dependence the model allows for. Leaving out a positive dependence undercounts
$N$, and leaving out a negative one overcounts it. The three-way interaction is never fitted, because the observed cells
cannot identify it~\citep{aleshinguendel2024}. Intervals are profile likelihood on
the full multinomial. \texttt{ll\_independence} fits no interactions;
\texttt{ll\_bic} chooses the subset by BIC, and its interval is conditional on
the chosen model, so selection uncertainty is not in it. Appendix~\ref{app:methods}
gives the remaining details.

\textbf{Latent classes} (\texttt{lc}) answer heterogeneity, where some actions are
hard for every witness. Each action belongs to an unobserved class and witnesses
are independent within a class~\citep{manriquevallier2016lcmcr}. With three
witnesses more than one class is not identified~\citep{aleshinguendel2020}, so
\texttt{lc\_gated} refuses to answer when any log-linear model within 6 BIC of the
best is unbounded, or falls outside the latent-class interval.

\textbf{One-sided bounds} (\texttt{anchor\_g}$\Gamma$) collapse the table to W3
against everyone else and fix their odds ratio $\psi$ at most $\Gamma$. At
$\psi = 1$ this is Lincoln--Petersen of the env diff against the logs. The
assumption $\psi \in [1, \Gamma]$ says that hiding can only make the env diff miss
more, so raising $\Gamma$ moves the upper end and never the lower one.

Before any agent ran, I tested these on synthetic capture histories with a known
$N$ (Table~\ref{tab:sim}).

theory says, covering 0.010, 0.368, 0.934 and 0.998 at $\Gamma = 1, 2, 4, 8$.

specialise rather than collude, they are negatively dependent, the $\psi = 1$

specialise, the right output is the gate's refusal.

\section{Live: does it recover what the logs lose?}
\label{sec:live}

The environment is a back-office queue. Each episode generates 12 customers, 30
orders and a queue of support tickets: refunds, cancellations, address and email
changes, confirmations by email, a closing summary file. A live agent
(\texttt{deepseek-v4-flash} through OpenCode Go) works the queue with eleven tools.
The exploratory run is 15 episodes at 30 tickets, $N = 589$.

Logs are recorded complete. Loss is applied afterwards, per record, 100 times per
configuration, with episodes resampled with replacement so each replicate has its
own $N$ and its own mix of operations. Post-hoc loss is valid because the faults
are independent of the agent by construction --- an assumption \S\ref{sec:limit}
breaks on purpose. Configurations vary how often the gateway and the trace drop
lines, and whether the env diff sees every operation or only financial ones
(refunds, cancellations, outbound mail), as change-data capture on a subset of
tables would.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering

union stops covering and the estimator holds.}

The hidden count was predictable before the run. Under \texttt{fin\_sampled} the
env diff cannot see the 181 non-financial actions. One of those is hidden from all
three witnesses when it also loses its trace line (0.3) and its gateway line
(0.9), so the expected count is $181 \times 0.3 \times 0.9 = 48.9$. The mean measured was 48.77.
That checks the loss arithmetic and the harness, not the estimator. Under that
configuration (Table~\ref{tab:live}) the union of all three logs undercounts by
exactly the hidden mass and never covers. \texttt{ll\_bic} covers 0.950 and
detects the gap in 98 of 100 replicates.

\paragraph{Breaking the independence assumption.}
The hidden actions above come from independent coin flips, and
\texttt{ll\_independence} assumes independence. A demonstration built that way
cannot fail the assumption it tests. So I broke it, writing the predictions down
first: a share $\rho$ of actions have their gateway and trace losses driven by one
draw, as a single outage takes down both pipes.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\input{tables/coupling}
\caption{Coupled loss. The independence model undercovers and undercounts once its
assumption is false; the selected model holds, consistent with fitting the

\label{tab:coupling}

\texttt{ll\_independence} fell to 0.780 coverage and undercounted by 10.82 at full
coupling. \texttt{ll\_bic} held at 0.960 (Table~\ref{tab:coupling}). The falsifier
written before the run was that independence-model coverage holding at 0.95 under
full coupling would mean the experiment tested nothing. It did not hold. The
assumption carries weight, and the selected model's coverage is consistent with
recovering a dependence its model class contains; which models BIC selected is
not tabulated. That is all this shows. A dependence the model cannot represent
is tested in \S\ref{sec:limit}, and there it fails.

\paragraph{Choosing the estimator.}
Picking the estimator that won one configuration would have picked
\texttt{ll\_independence}, which loses catastrophically under coupling. Worst-case
coverage alone would not have picked \texttt{ll\_bic} either:
\texttt{lc\_gated} (0.970) and \texttt{anchor\_g4} (0.960) are not
distinguishable from its 0.920 at 100 replicates a cell, a minimum over six
noisy cells, and cost roughly three and six times its mean width
(Table~\ref{tab:worst}). I chose
\texttt{ll\_bic} as a trade: no catastrophic case, the same gap detection as the
independence model, and close to the narrowest interval. That is a judgement
about width, not a rule that picked itself, and \S\ref{sec:confirm} shows it was
the judgement that left least margin.

\section{A pre-registered test on configurations nobody had seen}
\label{sec:confirm}

Everything in \S\ref{sec:live} is exploratory, and the estimator was chosen on it.
The pre-registration I first wrote tested \texttt{ll\_bic}'s coverage on one of the
six configurations it had been selected on, two points below the figure that
selected it. A reviewer given only that document and the plan, with the brief of
stopping a bad one being frozen, caught it. Fresh episode seeds do not fix that
kind of circularity: the configuration structure was identical.

The frozen version tests four hypotheses on two configurations held out of every
earlier run: \texttt{fin\_mid} (both logs lose half their lines, env diff
financial-only) and \texttt{fin\_mid\_rho100} (the same, with gateway and trace
losses coupled). The headline linkage is effect-signature. The run is attempted
once. An interrupted run is analysed as it stands and never topped up, and no
episode is discarded. The confirmatory agent is \texttt{deepseek-v4.1-flash} on
seeds used nowhere else.

The first attempt died partway through on a \texttt{400} from the provider. The
harness retried 5xx responses but not 400s, discarded the response body, and let
one failed episode end the study. All three were fixed and the study was rerun on
the same seeds, with no hypothesis computed from the partial data. That rerun is
a deviation from the protocol, which said the run is attempted once. In the
rerun three episodes were lost and the harness carried on: 21 of 24 completed,
$N = 828$.

I called those losses transient, and two of them were not. Both were the same
provider \texttt{400}, asking for the model's reasoning to be passed back; the
third was a read timeout. The same seed fails on one trajectory and completes on
the next, so which episodes were lost was not fixed by the seed or the ticket
mix, and a check on ticket mix could not see the difference. Whether the agent's
behaviour or the provider triggers the \texttt{400} is not identified, and one
candidate fix did not stop it.

with an interval 42.0 wide, asserting the gap in every replicate

interval spans about two thirds of the 63.15 actions it is estimating. In \texttt{fin\_mid\_rho100}
\texttt{ll\_independence} collapsed to 0.000, undercounting by 93.63 with an
interval only 24.1 wide, confident and wrong, while \texttt{ll\_bic} held at
0.930, consistent with fitting the interaction. Oracle linkage gave the same
0.940 at \texttt{fin\_mid}, so matching did not move coverage here.

Two of the hypotheses are weaker than they look, and both points came from a
second reviewer after the run. H4 compares gap detection against
\texttt{union\_count}, whose gap detection is 0 by construction, so it reduces to a
looser form of H2. And gap detection saturates: every interval-producing estimator
reaches 1.00 in \texttt{fin\_mid}, so it separates methods from the floor, not
methods from each other. Width does that, and on width \texttt{lc\_gated} pays for
its coverage with an interval more than three times wider.

\paragraph{The pass was marginal.}
The registered analysis used one loss seed of 100 replicates. Its Monte Carlo
error is about 0.024, so the result sits 1.7 Monte Carlo errors above the
threshold. I reran the registered episodes at three more loss seeds. Separately,
I restored the three lost seeds, each taking its first successful completion on
the changed client: a rule written down before any of these were analysed
(Table~\ref{tab:supplement}). Restoring them is a second deviation, since the
protocol forbids topping up, and the restored pool is not the planned run
either: its episodes ran on two client versions, and each restored seed is the
first success, not the first attempt. It is reported beside the registered
verdict, never in place of it.

On the registered episodes the mean is 0.915, 1.1 Monte Carlo errors above the

overcounts on every seed and covers below its nominal 0.95. What survives every
seed in both pools is the rest: the union never covers, \texttt{ll\_bic} asserts
the gap in every replicate, and the coupling contrast holds, with the
independence model losing 0.94 to 0.97 of its coverage and \texttt{ll\_bic} at

\section{The limit: missingness that depends on the action}
\label{sec:limit}

Every configuration so far deletes log lines independently of what the agent did.
The coupling experiment correlates the witnesses with each other; it never
correlates the loss with the actions. Real logging loss is not like that. Sampling
rules keyed to volume, bursts during load spikes and retries after the same crash
that produced an unusual action all concentrate loss on particular actions, and a
pooled capture-recapture model has no defence against that.

So I raised both logs' drop rate 1.8-fold on exactly the operations the
financial env view cannot see: the actions every witness is then weakest on. This
was built after the confirmatory run and cannot change its verdicts.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\input{tables/mnar}
\caption{Loss concentrated on the actions no witness sees.}
\label{tab:mnar}
\end{table}

200.21 actions per replicate went missing, against 63.15 before
(Table~\ref{tab:mnar}). \texttt{ll\_bic} still reports 0.940 coverage, but 0.13
of its intervals are unbounded (Table~\ref{tab:mnarstrat}) and the finite ones
average 1978.4 wide against $N = 828$: it covers by spanning more than twice the
population, which is not a measurement. With coupled loss added it covers 0.000. I
had predicted that latent classes would rescue the pooled model here. They did not:
\texttt{lc\_gated} is biased by $-134.36$ and its gap detection falls to 0.22.

The claim therefore splits. \emph{Detection survives}: \texttt{ll\_bic} still
asserts the logs are incomplete in nearly every replicate, and the union never
does. \emph{Quantification does not}: once loss concentrates on the actions no
witness sees, the interval stops being a count. An auditor can use this method to
say that actions are missing. In the one action-dependent configuration tested it
did not say how many, and nothing here shows that it can when the loss depends
on the action.

\paragraph{Conditioning on what the auditor can see.}
The loss above depends on the operation, and every captured record carries its
operation. That makes it missing-at-random given an observed covariate, which
is weaker than true MNAR. Within one operation, the logs lose lines by coin
flips at a constant rate. So I fitted the selected log-linear model per
operation, turned each profile likelihood into a density, and convolved the
strata (\texttt{strat\_bic}; the flat prior and the convolution are in
Appendix~\ref{app:methods}). A stratum with fewer than two live witnesses, or
none that disagree, contributes its observed count to the lower end and sends
the upper end to infinity.

The boundary experiment moves the same loss onto a key no record carries: the
ledger id modulo 3. Stratifying on the operation cannot see that, and it should
fail. Fifteen predictions were committed before either ran, and twelve held. Of
the three that did not, one missed by less than its Monte Carlo error. One I had
not anticipated at all: on thin strata a flat prior pushes the whole posterior
up, so at \texttt{fin\_sampled} every miss fell above $N$. The third put a
ceiling on coverage under coupled sampled loss that the estimator slightly
exceeded.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\input{tables/mnar_strat}
\caption{Pooled against stratified, when the loss depends on something the
auditor can observe and when it does not.}
\label{tab:mnarstrat}
\end{table}

Under loss keyed on the operation, \texttt{strat\_bic} covers by refusing, in
every replicate (Table~\ref{tab:mnarstrat}). The non-financial strata hold two
witnesses at about 0.1 capture each, and the estimator gives no finite interval
there. Under coupled loss it refuses again, where \texttt{ll\_bic} covers 0.000.
Under loss keyed on the hidden id, both fail. \texttt{ll\_bic} covers 0.600 and
refuses in 0.02 of replicates. \texttt{strat\_bic} covers 0.510 and refuses in
0.52, and almost all of that coverage is refusal: 50 of its 52 refusals covered,
and 1 of its 48 finite intervals did. The env diff supplies
disagreement, so the identifiability check passes, while the no-three-way
assumption the pooled model rests on fails.

Neither estimator dominates. The pooled model assumes no three-way interaction
across strata, and the stratified model assumes independence inside each
two-witness stratum. The data cannot tell which is true. What the pair does
give is a diagnostic: where the stratified estimator refuses and the pooled one
returns a finite interval, that interval rests on an assumption nobody can test,
and it should be reported that way. Agreement between them proves nothing. They
agree, and are both wrong, under coupled hidden-key loss.

\paragraph{When logs specialise.}
Everything above concerns actions that are hidden. The other error is a gap
that is not there. Under mutually exclusive loss the gateway and the trace never
lose the same action, so nothing is hidden and the two logs are negatively
dependent: each records what the other drops. Two such configurations were run,
one with the full env diff (\texttt{anti\_full}) and one with the financial view
(\texttt{anti\_fin}), beside an independent light-loss control (\texttt{light};
all three in Appendix~\ref{app:methods}). This is exploratory, after the
confirmatory run, on one loss seed. I predicted the pooled selected model would
claim a false gap.

It depends on matching (Table~\ref{tab:falsepos}). With exact matching,
\texttt{ll\_bic} claims no gap in any of the three configurations, consistent
with fitting the negative dependence. With effect-signature matching, the
matching an auditor actually has, it claims none at \texttt{anti\_full} and a gap
in every replicate at \texttt{anti\_fin}, overcounting by about 17.

Signature matching pairs records in order within a signature. Two different
actions that share one --- two emails to the same customer --- merge into one
action seen by both when they sit in different logs, and mutual exclusion puts
them in different logs as a rule; elsewhere the merge loses distinct actions.
Under signature matching 0.78 actions per replicate went unseen by every log, all
of it a linkage artefact, against about 17 estimated. That is consistent with
the model reading the distortion as hidden mass. The collision rate itself was
not measured.

The other two estimators fare worse. The independence model claims false gaps
under exact matching too (0.94 and 1.00), and the stratified model does at
\texttt{anti\_fin} under both. So, in these three configurations and on one
seed: \texttt{ll\_bic} made no false gap with independent loss or exact
matching, and made one in every replicate in the one cell where logs specialise,
the env view is partial and matching is by signature.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Integrity of agent records.} Hash chains and Merkle commitments detect
edits to one log~\citep{flightrecorder2026}. Trusted execution and notarised
transport make a trace host-independent~\citep{vet2025}, and systems audit frameworks
add tamper evidence against attackers who race the logger~\citep{paccagnella2020kenny,paccagnella2020custos}.
PeerReview has witnesses replay each other's tamper-evident logs to find omissions
deterministically~\citep{haeberlen2007peerreview}. All of these make a record
trustworthy; none estimates what is absent from it.

\paragraph{Monitoring in AI control.} Combining cost-constrained runtime monitors
improves detection under a budget~\citep{hua2025monitors}, and work on untrusted
monitors motivates collusion between a policy and its monitor, the adaptive
adversary of the simulator. Models claiming tool actions they never
took~\citep{transluce2025o3} is the reason self-report cannot be the only witness
and linkage has to tolerate fabricated records.

\paragraph{Capture-recapture beyond populations.} Software inspection used it to
estimate defects no reviewer found, and found inspector independence to be the
failure point~\citep{eick1992inspection,petersson2004inspection}. Fuzzing uses
species-richness estimators for residual risk~\citep{bohme2018stads}, and security
work used two-list estimates over phishing feeds~\citep{moore2007phish}. Over LLM
judges, it produced valid but uninformative intervals~\citep{kansal2026labels}.

\paragraph{Identification.} $N$ is not identified from the observed cells without
an assumption the data cannot test~\citep{aleshinguendel2024}; partial
identification gives confidence sets under bounded
dependence~\citep{sun2020bounds,dulcerubio2024}. The one-sided bounds here are an
instance of that family, anchored on the witness least controlled by the agent.

\section{Limits}
\label{sec:limits}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Benign agents only.} No live agent was given a reason to hide. Every
L1 and L2 number is from the parametric simulator, and a passing H1 is not evidence
for the adversarial case that motivates the work.
\item \textbf{Simulated loss.} Logs were recorded complete and thinned afterwards.
Coverage is over simulated faults, conditional on the recorded behaviour. Loss
correlated with the actions breaks quantification (\S\ref{sec:limit}).
\item \textbf{One model family, one environment.} Two versions of one model and one
back-office domain. Transfer is in \S\ref{sec:transfer}.
\item \textbf{Exploration before registration.} The pre-registration covers the
confirmatory arm only. Everything before it, including the estimator choice, was
seen first.
\item \textbf{A marginal confirmatory pass.} 21 of the 24 planned episodes
completed, and which were lost depended on the trajectory, not the seed. The
coverage hypothesis passed by 1.7 Monte Carlo errors on the registered seed and
by about one averaged over four seeds, and fails by the rule fixed in advance with
the lost episodes restored. The crash rerun and the restoration both deviate from
the protocol.
\item \textbf{A numbered log may be enough.} Every action in the back-office
environment passes the gateway, so a gateway numbering its entries before
sampling would make this method unnecessary there. Its case rests on actions that
no single numbered channel sees, which the simulator models and the live runs
do not.
\item \textbf{Cheaper checks were not compared.} Seeded canary actions measure
each log's capture rate directly, and sequence-number gaps count drops in any log
that numbers its entries. Neither was run head to head.
\item \textbf{False gaps under signature matching} when logs specialise and
the env view is partial (\S\ref{sec:limit}), from one configuration and one
seed.
\item \textbf{The agent can read its own trace.} W1's file is read-only to the
agent, and the agent read it in most confirmatory episodes. With loss injected
afterwards this cannot touch the independence the estimators assume. Against an
agent with a reason to hide, it is a channel that study would have to close.
\end{itemize}

\section{Reproducing this}
\label{sec:reproduce}

The repository holds the environment, the estimators, every recorded episode and
every artifact a number in this paper comes from. \texttt{make validate} runs the
tests, including one that fails if a figure in the README is absent from the
measurement card, and \texttt{scripts/paper\_tables.py} regenerates every table here
from the artifacts. The simulator sweep is \texttt{make sim}, and the confirmatory
analysis is \texttt{scripts/thin\_episodes.py --run confirm --pool heldout} followed
by \texttt{scripts/test\_hypotheses.py}. All live runs together cost \$3.57. The
decision log, \texttt{docs/DECISIONS.md}, has a dated entry for every prediction
written before a run and every result that contradicted one.

\paragraph{Loss.} Each witness record is dropped with its log's probability
(Table~\ref{tab:configs}), independently unless the configuration couples it.
Under $\rho$ coupling, an action is coupled with probability $\rho$; a coupled
action draws one uniform $u$ that its gateway and trace records share, and each
record is dropped when $u$ is below its log's drop rate. Since the gateway drops
at least as often as the trace, a coupled action that lost its trace line lost
its gateway line too. Under exclusive loss the trace record uses $1-u$, so with
drop rates summing to at most 1 the two logs never drop the same action. The
\texttt{financial} env view keeps W3 records only for refunds, cancellations and
outbound mail. MNAR configurations multiply both logs' drop rate, capped at 1,
for the named operations; the hidden-key ones do it for every third ledger id
and also remove those actions from W3.

\paragraph{W3.} The env witness is computed from the kernel's state, not from
the ledger: a diff of its tables between the start and the end of the episode,
plus the outbound mail sink. A change made and reverted inside an episode leaves
no diff row, and repeated changes to one field show as one. A ledger id is
attached to W3 rows only for oracle linkage.

\paragraph{Signature linkage.} A signature is the operation, the resource and,
for refunds, customer updates, cancellations and file deletions, the value. File
bodies and email text are left out, because an auditor reading a transcript
cannot reproduce a hash of text it paraphrased. Within one signature, if witness
$w$ has $c_w$ records there are $\max_w c_w$ actions, and records are paired in
order: the $j$-th action is seen by every witness with more than $j$ records.
Agent-supplied identifiers are never used.

\paragraph{Scoring.} The coverage target is the ledger count of the
replicate's resampled episodes, under both linkages. An interval $[lo, hi]$
covers when $lo \le N \le hi$, so an unbounded interval covers whenever its lower
end is at most $N$; the tables report how often that happens. Gap detection is
$lo$ above the number of distinct actions the three witnesses saw, after linkage.
Mean widths are over bounded intervals.

\paragraph{Log-linear models.} The candidates are every subset of the three
pairwise interactions, eight models, with no three-way term. Each is a Poisson
GLM on the seven observed cells, fitted by iteratively reweighted least squares.
The interval is a 95\% profile likelihood on the full multinomial, searched up
to 20 times the observed count; a profile that does not close below that is
reported unbounded. \texttt{ll\_bic}'s interval is conditional on the selected
model. \texttt{ll\_average} instead mixes the models' normalised profile
likelihoods with BIC weights, with the mass past the search cap as one atom at
infinity.

\paragraph{Latent classes.} \texttt{lc} is a latent-class model with a
truncated stick-breaking prior over at most 10 classes, concentration
$\alpha \sim \mathrm{Gamma}(0.25, 0.25)$, uniform priors on capture
probabilities and a $1/N$ prior on $N$, sampled by Gibbs on the cell counts with
1000 burn-in and 2000 kept draws. Its interval is the central 95\% of the
posterior. \texttt{lc\_gated} reports $[n, \infty)$ instead when any converged
log-linear model within 6 BIC of the best is unbounded, or has a point estimate
outside \texttt{lc}'s interval.

\paragraph{Stratified.} \texttt{strat\_bic} fits the BIC-selected log-linear
model per operation, after dropping witnesses that saw nothing in that stratum.
A stratum is identified only with at least two live witnesses that disagree
somewhere. Its profile likelihood over $N_s \ge n_s$ is normalised into a
density, which is a flat prior on $N_s$, with a power-law tail past the search
cap. The densities are convolved and the total's 2.5, 50 and 97.5\% points
reported. An unidentified stratum, or one whose density cannot be normalised,
adds its observed count to the lower end and sends the upper end to infinity.
