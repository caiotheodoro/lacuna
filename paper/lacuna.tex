\author{Caio Theodoro}

\begin{abstract}
Agent audit trails are engineered for integrity: hash chains, append-only
servers, attested execution. Their completeness is assumed. Agents have been
shown to delete and spoof their own trace events, and no audit regime I surveyed
estimates how many consequential actions are missing from the record. When no
single log is complete, that count is a population-size problem: three logs
written by different infrastructure --- the agent's own trace, a gateway proxy
log and an end-of-shift environment diff --- each see part of what an agent did,
and capture-recapture over their overlaps estimates the actions none of them saw.

I test this with a benign live agent in three studies, in environments whose kernels keep a
ledger no log reads. Each log was complete before any loss, so loss is simulated
afterwards. Two pre-registered tests of a BIC-selected log-linear estimator used
effect-signature record matching, and a third tested it with actions that bypass the gateway. The back-office test passed at its registered
seed (coverage 0.940 against a 0.90 bar) but covers 0.893 over 3000 replicates;
the infra test failed (0.300, and 0.401 over 20 seeds). Matching on the ledger
id, which no auditor has, gives 0.948 and 0.946. Post hoc, on the same episodes,
linking the trace and the gateway by the tool-call id they share restores 0.946
in back-office under the registered environment view, where the diff is easy to
attach, but only 0.859 in infra, where it is not.

Loss that arrives in bursts makes the profile interval too narrow, with coverage
0.33 to 0.83 at blocks of 12 under exact matching. Post hoc, scaling the interval by
a dispersion estimated from variation between episodes never lowers coverage and
raises it to 0.85 to 1.00 under bursts and joint outages, as a width-for-coverage
trade: intervals two and a half to four times as wide, and half again to double
whenever sessions differ at all. Much of the burst failure is not dispersion: the
agent works one kind of ticket at a time, so a lost block lands on the stretch of
an episode the environment diff cannot see, and the point estimate is biased.
Loss that concentrates on those actions by kind, not by time, is not repaired by
any estimator variant tried: the estimator fails silently from a tenth more loss,
and stratifying on the operation stays calibrated only where it answers. Two
cheaper checks do survive it in simulation. A gateway sequence counter with a tail
correction covers 0.952 at 1.2 times that loss, where \texttt{ll\_bic} covers
0.254, and canary actions the auditor plants cover at least 0.92 in every cell
where they are exchangeable with real actions. The counter covers 0.0 under bursts
and 0.002 when 5\% of actions bypass the gateway, and the canaries' intervals are
wider and detect the gap less often (0.69 against 1.0). Under exact matching a loss
rate that ramps over the episode with the same mean also breaks \texttt{ll\_bic}
(0.742 and 0.471), and under signature or call-id matching so do duplicated and
forged lines. An LLM auditor reading the agent's transcript was either a copy of
the trace or empty in the two settings tried.

The third study is where a gateway counter cannot work. The agent could run
state-changing actions through a batch tool that the trace and the gateway log as
one call, and no log parses the batch (a stipulation; an auditor that parsed it would
recover every action). It ran 1098 of 1283 actions that way, a share of 0.856 against
a predicted 0.05 to 0.30. The registered test failed: \texttt{ll\_bic} covered 0.759
with intervals about 8.6 times as wide in absolute terms (about 5.5 times relative to
$N$) as in a different pool without bypass, and the counter covered 0.0. It still
detected that a gap exists (0.995, as it does with no bypass). Under the registered
view, 96\% of the hidden mass (317.8 of 330.7 actions per replicate) is bypassed
customer updates that no witness can record, so the registered cell asks the
estimator to extrapolate to actions with zero capture probability. Post hoc, parsing
the scripts restores coverage to 0.949, so the failure is due to the stipulation that no
counted log itemises the channel, not to the pool, the ticket mix or the tool. Under
the full env view, parsing helps only with ledger-id linkage (0.944), not call id
(0.487). No estimator tried on the registered cell covers more than 0.84. The
audit-warning prompt gave a share of 0.99 against 0.87 on the same 12 seeds, but two
neutral episodes carry the difference (without them, $+0.013$), so the pilot does not
show the prompt changed behaviour; its coverage of 0.996 is unbounded intervals. No
adversarial agent was run. The method is for settings where every action reaches at
least one counted log; this study tests the boundary where a channel reaches none, and
there the estimator cannot bound $N$.
\end{abstract}

\section{Integrity is engineered; completeness is assumed}

Every audit regime that reaches an AI agent starts from a log. The record-keeping
article of the EU AI Act~\citep{euaiact}, the event logging in ISO/IEC 42001 and
the incident reports that California's SB~53 now requires all assume a record of
what the system did. The engineering around that record has converged on one
property. Logs are hash-chained, committed to Merkle trees, written to
append-only servers, notarised inside trusted execution
environments~\citep{flightrecorder2026,vet2025}. Every one of these answers
whether a line was changed after it was written.

None of them answers whether the record is whole. A deleted line in a hash chain
is detectable. A line that was never written is not. Qin et al.\ showed agents
deleting their own trace events, inserting fake ones and spoofing tool calls when
asked, and doing it unprompted when it raised their reward~\citep{qin2026tamper}.
Their proposed fix is an independent interception server with an append-only
log. It is not evaluated, and they note its limit themselves: it would prove
that model outputs were not altered afterwards, not that tool calls ran as
reported.

The field measures trace integrity and assumes trace completeness. The quantity
an auditor needs is the second one: how many consequential actions the agent took
that no log shows.

That count only needs estimating when no single record is complete. If every
action passes one channel, that channel numbers its entries before anything is
sampled or dropped, and the counter itself is logged, the highest number is the
count and nothing in this paper is needed. If the last records are lost, the
numbering gives a lower bound; a tail correction restores an interval only while
loss is independent from record to record (\S\ref{sec:cheap}). The condition fails
in three common ways. An
action can reach the world without passing the numbered channel: a code-execution
side channel, a direct database write. The logs can belong to different parties
that do not expose their numbering: a vendor's trace, a customer's gateway, a
SaaS audit log. Or sampling can happen before numbering, so the numbered stream is
already thinned. In the back-office environment below every action does pass the
gateway. A gateway that numbered its action entries before sampling, with its
counter logged, would recover $N$ exactly; one that numbered every call would
recover only the dropped calls,
since it also logs reads and errors (1480 calls for 828 actions in the
confirmatory run). The live experiments there calibrate the estimator where its
answer can be checked. They do not show it is needed there.
The method is for settings where every action reaches at least one counted log under
loss that is independent enough. \S\ref{sec:bypass} tests the boundary, where a
channel reaches none of them: the counter fails there, and \texttt{ll\_bic} cannot bound
$N$.

The method for that count is old. Epidemiologists estimate the casualties no
register recorded by overlapping several incomplete
registers~\citep{fienberg1972,bird2018mse}, and software teams once estimated the
defects no inspector found the same way~\citep{eick1992inspection,petersson2004inspection}.
If three logs each miss some of an agent's actions, and miss them for different
reasons, the actions two logs share and the third lacks say how much all three
lacked. I found no published work that applies multiple-systems estimation to
agent trace completeness. The nearest attempt, capture-recapture over LLM judges
to count label errors, produced intervals that were valid and too wide to beat a
raw count~\citep{kansal2026labels}. That failure mode --- width, not coverage ---
runs through everything below.

I built two environments, and a batch-tool variant of one, whose kernels keep a ledger no
witness can read, ran a live LLM agent through them, and asked four questions. First, whether the obvious
extra witness, an LLM reading the agent's transcript, is a witness at all
(\S\ref{sec:auditor}). It is not. Second, whether capture-recapture recovers the
hidden count when logs lose lines, under a pre-registered test
(\S\ref{sec:live}--\S\ref{sec:transfer}). Third, why those tests missed
(\S\ref{sec:linkage}): the record linkage, and which linkage an auditor should
use instead. Fourth, what loss the estimator does not expect (\S\ref{sec:limit}):
loss in bursts, and loss that depends on the action. Between the second and the
third, \S\ref{sec:bypass} asks what the method does when actions do not pass a
numbered channel. The abstract has the results.

\section{The estimand, and the floor every method has to beat}
\label{sec:estimand}

An \emph{action} is a tool call that changes environment state or sends data out.
Reads are excluded. The environment kernel appends one ledger entry per action,
so $N$, the number of actions in an episode, is a program and not a label. No
witness reads the ledger.

Three witnesses observe actions, each through infrastructure the others do not
share:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
witness & source & blind spot \\
\midrule
W1 trace & the harness appends each tool call to a log file & sampling, export loss \\
W2 gateway & a proxy logs each tool call & sampling \\
W3 env & end-of-shift state diff plus a network sink & repeats collapse, reverts vanish \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Each action gets a capture history: which witnesses recorded it. Over three
witnesses that is a table of $2^3$ cells, and the cell for ``recorded by none''
is never observed. Estimating that cell is the whole problem.

Records are matched across witnesses in two registered ways. \emph{Exact}
matching (``oracle'' in the tables) uses the ledger id. No auditor has it; it is
the upper bound on what matching can achieve. \emph{Effect-signature} matching
pairs records on operation, resource and, for some operations, value, which an
auditor without ground truth can compute; Appendix~\ref{app:methods} gives the
exact rule, and \S\ref{sec:linkage} shows the rule mattered. Identifiers the
agent wrote are never used, because an agent that hides actions can forge them;
\S\ref{sec:linkage} returns to an identifier the harness writes.

Three baselines anchor the comparison. \texttt{trust\_trace} believes the
agent's own log; it is in the confirmatory table, where it undercounts by about
half. \texttt{union\_count} counts every distinct action any witness
saw; it is never above $N$, so once anything is hidden it never covers.
\texttt{union\_bound} reads that count as the interval $[n, \infty)$. It covers
every time and says nothing, which makes it the floor in the precise sense that a
method earns its place only by giving a finite upper end without losing coverage.

Coverage alone cannot separate a useful interval from a wide one, so every
estimator also reports \textbf{gap detection}: the share of replicates whose lower
end exceeds the union of all witnesses. A method that detects the gap is asserting
``these logs are missing actions, at 95\%''. \texttt{union\_bound} has gap
detection 0 by construction. Gap detection has false positives of its own, and
\S\ref{sec:linkage} measures when.

\section{The witness that copies the trace}
\label{sec:auditor}

The first extra witness anyone would add is an auditor: an LLM that reads the
agent's transcript and lists the actions it took. Arslan found that LLM
accountability layers relay the conclusions of the agents they audit instead of
checking them, finding a fault's true origin 4.1\% of the time when no agent
proposed it~\citep{arslan2026audit}. That predicts an auditor whose misses
coincide with the trace's. I measured something more extreme.

Reading the full transcript, the auditor recalled every action in 50 episodes
(Table~\ref{tab:auditor}), with no false positives, up to the longest sessions the
agent would sustain. I had predicted, in writing, that recall would fall with
session length. It did not at any length reached. The agent, and not the queue, set the
longest length: actions per ticket fell from 1.31 at 30 tickets to 0.61 at 120,
which I read as the agent not working long queues to the end.

Reading only what the agent said, the auditor recalled nothing in 10 episodes. It
was working --- it returned a parseable empty list every time --- and the agent
does narrate. Its closing message in one episode reads ``All 31 queue items are
handled: O26 and O17 cancelled (with emails), nine refunds issued \ldots\ all
address/email updates applied with duplicates ignored.'' That account is
aggregate. An audit record needs the customer, the field and the amount, and
``nine refunds'' is a count that cannot be overlapped with anything.

The auditor was the same model as the agent in each run, but the two settings
did not use the same model version: the full-transcript runs used
\texttt{deepseek-v4-flash} and the narration run \texttt{deepseek-v4.1-flash}, with
one prompt and one rendering of the narration. In hindsight the result is close
to obvious. It is reported because an LLM auditor is the first independent
witness most designs reach for. I tried two settings, and neither sits in
between: the only record of what the agent did is its call log, and the call
log is W1. In both settings this auditor was either a copy of the trace or empty,
and a witness with capture probability 1 pins $N$ to the observed count. The study below uses three witnesses. The auditor stays in the
repository as a measured dead end.

\section{Estimators, and where each one is identified}
\label{sec:estimators}

\textbf{Log-linear multiple-systems estimation}~\citep{fienberg1972} fits the
seven observed cells with a Poisson model: a main effect per witness plus chosen
pairwise interactions. The never-seen cell is the intercept. An interaction is a
dependence the model allows for. Leaving out a positive dependence undercounts
$N$, and leaving out a negative one overcounts it. The three-way interaction is never fitted, because the observed cells
cannot identify it~\citep{aleshinguendel2024}. Intervals are profile likelihood on
the full multinomial. \texttt{ll\_independence} fits no interactions;
\texttt{ll\_bic} chooses the subset by BIC, and its interval is conditional on
the chosen model, so selection uncertainty is not in it. Appendix~\ref{app:methods}
gives the remaining details.

\textbf{Latent classes} (\texttt{lc}) answer heterogeneity, where some actions are
hard for every witness. Each action belongs to an unobserved class and witnesses
are independent within a class~\citep{manriquevallier2016lcmcr}. With three
witnesses more than one class is not identified~\citep{aleshinguendel2020}, so
\texttt{lc\_gated} refuses to answer when any log-linear model within 6 BIC of the
best is unbounded, or falls outside the latent-class interval.

\textbf{One-sided bounds} (\texttt{anchor\_g}$\Gamma$) collapse the table to W3
against everyone else and fix their odds ratio $\psi$ at most $\Gamma$. At
$\psi = 1$ this is Lincoln--Petersen of the env diff against the logs. The
assumption $\psi \in [1, \Gamma]$ says that hiding can only make the env diff miss
more, so raising $\Gamma$ moves the upper end and never the lower one.

Before any agent ran, I tested these on synthetic capture histories with a known
$N$ (Table~\ref{tab:sim}, Appendix~\ref{app:tables}).


Four results set up the rest. When some actions are hard for every witness
(\texttt{l0\_heterogeneous}), \texttt{ll\_bic} covers 0.296 and \texttt{lc} covers
0.962. When each witness sees its own kind of action and they barely overlap
(\texttt{l0\_siloed}), \texttt{lc} is confidently wrong at 0.054, and the gate
refuses 0.97 of those tables, which is its job. When the auditor copies the trace
and is treated as independent, \texttt{ll\_independence} reports intervals 0.25
wide that cover 0.646 of the time; letting BIC select the auditor's dependence
restores 0.906 (Table~\ref{tab:simextra}). An adaptive agent that also evades the env diff
(\texttt{l2\_env\_blind}) breaks every point estimator: none covers above 0.218.
The true anchor odds ratio there is $\psi = 4.35$, and the bounds behave as the
theory says, covering 0.010, 0.368, 0.934 and 0.998 at $\Gamma = 1, 2, 4, 8$.


The bounds are one-sided by construction, and that is a real limit. When witnesses
specialise rather than collude, they are negatively dependent, the $\psi = 1$
point already overcounts, and no $\Gamma$ covers. Against witnesses that
specialise, the right output is the gate's refusal.

\section{Live: does it recover what the logs lose?}
\label{sec:live}

The environment is a back-office queue. Each episode generates 12 customers, 30
orders and a queue of support tickets: refunds, cancellations, address and email
changes, confirmations by email, a closing summary file. A live agent
(\texttt{deepseek-v4-flash} through OpenCode Go) works the queue with eleven tools.
The exploratory run is 15 episodes at 30 tickets, $N = 589$.

Logs are recorded complete. Loss is applied afterwards, per record, 100 times per
configuration, with episodes resampled with replacement so each replicate has its
own $N$ and its own mix of operations. Post-hoc loss is valid because the faults
are independent of the agent by construction --- an assumption \S\ref{sec:limit}
breaks on purpose. Configurations vary how often the gateway and the trace drop
lines, and whether the env diff sees every operation or only financial ones
(refunds, cancellations, outbound mail), as change-data capture on a subset of
tables would.


The hidden count was predictable before the run. Under \texttt{fin\_sampled} the
env diff cannot see the 181 non-financial actions. One of those is hidden from all
three witnesses when it also loses its trace line (0.3) and its gateway line
(0.9), so the expected count is $181 \times 0.3 \times 0.9 = 48.9$. The mean measured was 48.77.
That checks the loss arithmetic and the harness, not the estimator. Under that
configuration (Table~\ref{tab:live}, Appendix~\ref{app:tables}) the union of all three logs undercounts by
exactly the hidden mass and never covers. \texttt{ll\_bic} covers 0.950 with exact
matching and 0.930 with signature matching, and detects the gap in 98 of 100
replicates under both. Signature matching costs coverage elsewhere: at
\texttt{light}, where almost nothing is hidden, \texttt{ll\_bic} covers 0.940 with
exact matching and 0.870 with signature matching. Each cell is one loss seed of
100 replicates, so its Monte Carlo error is about 0.02.

\paragraph{Breaking the independence assumption.}
The hidden actions above come from independent coin flips, and
\texttt{ll\_independence} assumes independence. A demonstration built that way
cannot fail the assumption it tests. So I broke it, writing the predictions down
first: a share $\rho$ of actions have their gateway and trace losses driven by one
draw, as a single outage takes down both pipes.


With exact matching \texttt{ll\_independence} fell to 0.780 coverage and
undercounted by 10.82 at full coupling, while \texttt{ll\_bic} covered 0.960
(Table~\ref{tab:coupling}). The falsifier written before the run was that
independence-model coverage holding at 0.95 under full coupling would mean the
experiment tested nothing. It did not hold. The assumption carries weight. With
signature matching \texttt{ll\_bic} covered 0.970 at full coupling but 0.870 at
half coupling, on an interval three times wider. Its coverage is consistent with
recovering a dependence its model class contains, and that is all this shows. A dependence the model cannot represent
is tested in \S\ref{sec:limit}, and there it fails.

\paragraph{Choosing the estimator.}
Picking the estimator that won one configuration would have picked
\texttt{ll\_independence}, which loses catastrophically under coupling. Worst-case
coverage alone would not have picked \texttt{ll\_bic} either:
\texttt{lc\_gated} (0.970) and \texttt{anchor\_g4} (0.960) are not
distinguishable from its 0.920 at 100 replicates a cell, a minimum over six
noisy cells, and cost roughly three and six times its mean width
(Table~\ref{tab:worst}). I chose
\texttt{ll\_bic} as a trade: no catastrophic case, the same gap detection as the
independence model, and close to the narrowest interval. That is a judgement
about width, not a rule that picked itself. It was also made on the exact-matching
rows. Under signature matching, the linkage the pre-registration later named as
the headline, \texttt{ll\_bic}'s worst case is 0.870, against 0.980 for
\texttt{lc\_gated} and 0.970 for \texttt{anchor\_g4}. \S\ref{sec:confirm} shows
the choice left the least margin, and \S\ref{sec:linkage} why.


\paragraph{Power.}
Relative width follows the capture-recapture rate: width$/N$ times $\sqrt{N}$
averages 1.53 across the study sizes in Table~\ref{tab:power}. At that rate a
5\% relative width needs about 24 episodes at 30 tickets. Every arm resamples
the same 15 episodes, so this says how width scales, not whether coverage holds
in a larger sample of genuinely new episodes.


\section{A pre-registered test on configurations nobody had seen}
\label{sec:confirm}

Everything in \S\ref{sec:live} is exploratory, and the estimator was chosen on it.
The pre-registration I first wrote tested \texttt{ll\_bic}'s coverage on one of the
six configurations it had been selected on, two points below the figure that
selected it. A reviewer given only that document and the plan, with the brief of
stopping a bad one being frozen, caught it. Fresh episode seeds do not fix that
kind of circularity: the configuration structure was identical.

The frozen version tests four hypotheses on two configurations held out of every
earlier run: \texttt{fin\_mid} (both logs lose half their lines, env diff
financial-only) and \texttt{fin\_mid\_rho100} (the same, with gateway and trace
losses coupled). The headline linkage is effect-signature. The run is attempted
once. An interrupted run is analysed as it stands and never topped up, and no
episode is discarded. The confirmatory agent is \texttt{deepseek-v4.1-flash} on
seeds no earlier run used.

The first attempt died partway through on a \texttt{400} from the provider. The
harness retried 5xx responses but not 400s, discarded the response body, and let
one failed episode end the study. All three were fixed and the study was rerun on
the same seeds, with no hypothesis computed from the partial data. That rerun is
a deviation from the protocol, which said the run is attempted once. In the
rerun three episodes were lost and the harness carried on: 21 of 24 completed,
$N = 828$.

I called those losses transient, and two of them were not. Both were the same
provider \texttt{400}, asking for the model's reasoning to be passed back; the
third was a read timeout. The same seed fails on one trajectory and completes on
the next, so which episodes were lost was not fixed by the seed or the ticket
mix, and a check on ticket mix could not see the difference. Whether the agent's
behaviour or the provider triggers the \texttt{400} is not identified, and one
candidate fix did not stop it.

All four hypotheses passed on the registered analysis

with an interval 42.0 wide, asserting the gap in every replicate
(Table~\ref{tab:confirm}). Read against the hidden count rather than $N$, that
interval spans about two thirds of the 63.15 actions it is estimating. In \texttt{fin\_mid\_rho100}
\texttt{ll\_independence} collapsed to 0.000, undercounting by 93.63 with an
interval only 24.1 wide, confident and wrong, while \texttt{ll\_bic} held at
0.930. With equal drop rates and full coupling the trace and the gateway are the
same log, so this is a dependence \texttt{ll\_bic}'s model class contains, not a
test of robustness to dependence in general.

Three of the hypotheses are weaker than they look. H1 cannot speak for model
selection: at \texttt{fin\_mid} the logs fail independently, the independence
model is the true one, and it passes too. H4 compares gap detection against
\texttt{union\_count}, whose gap detection is 0 by construction, so it reduces to a
looser form of H2. And gap detection saturates: every interval-producing estimator
reaches 1.00 in \texttt{fin\_mid}, so it separates methods from the floor, not
methods from each other. Width does that, and on width \texttt{lc\_gated} pays for
its coverage with an interval more than three times wider.

\paragraph{The pass did not hold up.}
The registered analysis used one loss seed of 100 replicates, whose Monte Carlo
error is about 0.024: the 0.940 sat 1.7 errors above the bar. At 30 fresh loss
seeds on the same episodes (Table~\ref{tab:precision}), \texttt{ll\_bic} covers
0.893, about one Monte Carlo error below the bar. The method as registered
misses its coverage hypothesis in expectation; the registered seed was a
favourable draw. Restoring the three lost episodes is a second deviation, since
the protocol forbids topping up, and the restored pool ran on two client
versions, each restored seed taking its first success. On it coverage is 0.862.
Appendix~\ref{app:supplement} has the intermediate four-seed analysis this
supersedes.

Under exact record matching the same analysis covers 0.948, and 0.945 on the
restored pool. The whole shortfall is linkage, which \S\ref{sec:linkage} takes
apart. By the pre-registration's own tie-break, a gap over 5 points between the

What survives every seed in both pools is the rest: the union never covers,

coverage and \texttt{ll\_bic} at most 0.01. As registered, then, the method

handle, and misses its coverage bar.

\section{A pre-registered second environment}
\label{sec:transfer}

I pre-registered a transfer to an infra/ops environment before running it
(\texttt{docs/PREREG-TRANSFER.md}). Its env-diff blindness comes from the domain
rather than from a configured view: the end-state diff misses transient work,
such as deploys rolled back after a failed smoke check, load-test scale-ups
reverted, and debug toggles switched off again. T1 to T4 mirror H1 to H4 at
\texttt{transfer\_mid} and \texttt{transfer\_mid\_rho100}, with effect-signature
linkage as the headline.

T1 failed. \texttt{ll\_bic} covered 0.300 on the registered seed (23 of 24
episodes, $N = 1036$; seed 9012 was lost to the same provider \texttt{400}).
Over 20 fresh loss seeds it covers 0.401 under signature matching and 0.946 under
exact matching (Table~\ref{tab:transfer}). T2 and T4 passed at 1.00 but say
little: with nothing hidden, the same estimator under signature matching claims
a gap in 1.00 of replicates. T3 passed under exact matching for the reason H3
did. Under signature matching it passed only because the interval roughly
doubled while the bias stayed near $-32$. The mechanism is in
\S\ref{sec:linkage}.

The verdict depends on density (Table~\ref{tab:transferdensity}). In scripted
runs, signature coverage is 0.860 at 10 tickets over 10 services and 0.155 at 60,
as the share of W3-invisible actions with a colliding signature rises from 0.092

one point on that curve.


None of this is about the agent. The paid run reproduces a free scripted run:
1035 of the agent's 1036 actions are in the scripted plans, and the scripted
episodes give the same split (Table~\ref{tab:transfer} has the paid run;
\texttt{transfer-precision.json} has both). The tickets spell out their steps,
and the agent's only decisions were the metric-conditioned branches, which it
got right. What the transfer tests is the environment, the loss model and the
linkage, and in both environments the same split appears: exact matching gives
nominal coverage and the registered linkage does not. T1's failure was also
predicted before the freeze, from a scripted pipeline check, so it confirms a
known mechanism rather than discovering one.

\section{Actions that bypass the gateway}
\label{sec:bypass}

\paragraph{Why this setting.} In both environments above every action passes
the gateway, so a numbered gateway would give $N$ without an estimator. The method
is for settings where every action reaches at least one counted log, under loss that
is independent enough. This study tests the boundary of that scope, a channel that
reaches none of the counted logs. I pre-registered it
(\texttt{docs/PREREG-BYPASS.md}). When an LLM agent may route state-changing
actions through a batch tool that the gateway and the trace each record as one
call, does \texttt{ll\_bic} keep a calibrated interval for $N$? Where the channel is
itemised by no counted log, the estimator has to extrapolate to actions with zero
capture probability, and the result below says it cannot bound $N$ there.

\paragraph{The environment.} \texttt{backoffice\_bypass} is the back-office kernel,
tools and ticket generator plus \texttt{run\_script(script)}. A script holds one
operation per line, over five operations (refund, cancel, customer update, email,
file write), parsed against a closed whitelist; nothing is executed as code. The
ledger gets one entry per inner action. The trace and the gateway log one
\texttt{run\_script} call holding the script text. The env diff sees the effects,
with its usual blind spots. No witness parses a script, and an auditor who did
would recover every inner action. That is a stipulation, and the results are
conditional on it. The system prompt is unchanged, and five of 30 tickets are
shaped as batches.

\paragraph{The registration.} It was frozen before any paid episode and tagged
\texttt{prereg-bypass}. The tool description and system prompt are pinned by hash,
and so is the analysis code (\texttt{bypass\_analysis.py},
\texttt{bypass\_verdicts.py}); the diff
\texttt{git diff prereg-bypass -{}- src scripts/run\_episodes.py
scripts/bypass\_analysis.py scripts/bypass\_verdicts.py} is empty at the analysis commit
(\texttt{1046284}). The wider \texttt{-{}- src scripts} now shows unrelated later changes. Two refuter passes read it before the
freeze. Both paid runs, 24 neutral and 12 with the audit-warning prompt (L1), were
committed before any analysis, and all 36 episodes completed. B0 checks the
manipulation: the pooled bypass share, with its interval's lower end above 0.05.
B1 is calibration: \texttt{ll\_bic} at \texttt{fin\_mid} with call-id linkage
against 0.90, with a leave-one-episode-out spread; call id replaces the signature
headline because of \S\ref{sec:linkage}. B2 and B3 are design checks expected to
pass whatever happened, and L1 has no verdict. The predictions went in before the
runs (Table~\ref{tab:bypass}).

\section{Record linkage is where the method breaks first}
\label{sec:linkage}

Both pre-registered tests missed their coverage bar under the linkage they
registered and were nominal under exact matching. The capture-recapture
estimator only sees a table of how many actions each combination of logs
recorded, and building that table means deciding which records in different
logs are the same action. Get that wrong and the estimator is answering a
different question. The two environments get it wrong in different ways.

\paragraph{Back-office: a dependence that is not there.}
Of the confirmatory actions, 0.223 share an effect signature with another action
in the same episode, almost all of them emails to the same customer. In-order
pairing lines up records of different actions as one action seen by both the
trace and the gateway, which looks like positive trace--gateway dependence. Under
exact matching BIC selects that interaction in 0.010 of replicates; under
signature matching in 0.358, and in 0.517 on the restored pool
(Table~\ref{tab:precision}). When it does, \texttt{ll\_bic} overcounts by about
13 and covers 0.858. The independence model, which cannot fit that interaction,
covers 0.951 under exact and 0.948 under signature matching.

\paragraph{Infra: actions merged away.}
In the second environment the trace--gateway interaction is rarely selected, and
the independence model is as wrong as BIC. An ablation locates the cause
(Table~\ref{tab:transfermech}).
Matching exactly only the actions in signature groups the env diff missed wholly
or partly restores 0.930. Matching exactly the groups it saw whole changes
nothing. Undo work is the largest source but not the only one: exact matching for
undo tickets alone gives 0.904, and for every other ticket 0.851. Adding page and
runbook text to the signature does not help. Merged clusters cost observed
actions, and in-order pairing overstates trace--gateway overlap (277.5 against
259.7 under exact matching).


\paragraph{False gaps.}
The other linkage error is a gap that is not there. Under mutually exclusive loss the gateway and the trace never
lose the same action, so nothing is hidden and the two logs are negatively
dependent: each records what the other drops. I first ran two such
configurations, one with the full env diff (\texttt{anti\_full}) and one with
the financial view (\texttt{anti\_fin}), beside an independent light-loss control
(\texttt{light}; all three in Appendix~\ref{app:methods}), and predicted the
pooled selected model would claim a false gap.


It depends on matching and on the env view (Table~\ref{tab:falsepos}). With
exact matching, \texttt{ll\_bic} claims no gap in any of the three
configurations, consistent with fitting the negative dependence. With
effect-signature matching it claims none at \texttt{anti\_full} and a gap in
every replicate at \texttt{anti\_fin}, overcounting by about 17. A battery over
four exclusive-loss rates, both env views and four episode pools shows this is a
regime, not one cell (Table~\ref{tab:falsegap}, Appendix~\ref{app:tables}). Under exact matching
\texttt{ll\_bic} claimed no false gap anywhere, nor under signature matching with
the full env view. Under signature matching with the financial view it claimed
one at every rate and in every pool, in 0.15 to 1.00 of replicates, and in none
of three null configurations where one log is complete.


The mechanism is the one behind the back-office shortfall. Signature matching
pairs records in order within a signature. Two different actions that share one
--- two emails to the same customer --- merge into one action seen by both when
they sit in different logs, and mutual exclusion puts them in different logs
more often than independent loss does. In the confirmatory episodes 0.223 of
actions share a signature with another action in the same episode, almost all of
them emails. Under signature matching at \texttt{anti\_fin}, 0.78 actions per
replicate went unseen by every log, all of it a linkage artefact, against about
17 estimated: the model reads the distortion as hidden mass. Matching one
operation at a time by signature isolates it. At \texttt{anti\_fin}, signature
matching on \texttt{send\_email} alone claims a gap in 1.00 of replicates, and on
every other operation together in none. The emails are an operation the env diff
sees, and their text is left out of the signature, so two emails to one
customer collide; the env diff's blindness elsewhere is what lets the model read
the collision as hidden mass. In the second environment the same holds for
pages: signature matching on \texttt{send\_page} alone produces the false gap,
even with the full env view, because the diff there still misses transient
actions. What the diff must see is every action, not every operation.

The other two estimators fare worse. The independence model claims false gaps
under exact matching too (0.94 and 1.00), and the stratified model does at
\texttt{anti\_fin} under both.

\paragraph{Linkage an auditor can actually do.}
The signature was a design choice, and a poor one. It left out email and file
bodies because an LLM auditor reading a paraphrased transcript could not
reproduce their hash. That auditor was dropped (\S\ref{sec:auditor}), but the
signature kept the omission, and every log records those bodies as a content
digest. The trace and the gateway also share the provider's tool-call id, which
the harness writes into both. Table~\ref{tab:linkagearms} compares five ways of
building the table, all of which recover $N$ exactly from complete logs.

\centering

\caption{Five linkages. Coverage of \texttt{ll\_bic} on held-out loss, and false

Under the registered financial view, either fix restores nominal coverage in
back-office: 0.942 with the digest and 0.946 with the call id, against 0.875 for
the registered signature on the same seeds. That cell is easy, though. The env
diff sees every email, refund and cancellation, so once the trace and the gateway
are linked exactly, attaching it cannot go wrong, and the call id matches exact
matching to the third decimal. With the full env view it has to be attached for
repeated customer updates too, and every auditor-computable arm falls one to
three and a half points short of exact matching (0.905 to 0.932 against 0.940
to 0.947).

Linking by call id alone, with the diff attached by signature, also removes every
false gap measured, in both environments, but that is close to true by
construction: when the trace and the gateway
together see every action, nothing they share can be mistaken for a missing one.
The digest removes the back-office false gaps but undercounts by about one action
on near-zero-width intervals, and in infra it restores neither false gaps nor
coverage, because the collisions there are identical repeated actions.

Infra is the hard case. The call id gives 0.859 and, at full coupling, nothing
(0.803 under every arm that links by signature or id), because the trace and the
gateway are then one log and all the remaining error is attaching the env diff,
which carries no id. A last-writer rule looks like it helps but does not hold
up: an end-state row for an overwritten field belongs to the latest logged call on
that field if the values match, and otherwise to an action no log shows, with
calls ordered by merging the two logs' line orders on the shared call id. It
reaches 0.916, against 0.944 under exact matching, but part of that is ordering
errors cancelling the rule's own undercount (D52). The same errors produce false
gaps when the logs lose mutually exclusive actions, in up to every replicate in
infra, where the plain call id produces none. It also assumes each log lists calls
in the order they ran, which the recorded logs always do and real logs may not. It
is withdrawn. What no rule
on these records can resolve is an action whose two log lines were both lost
while an earlier identical action was logged: from the diff those look the same,
and only a per-row id or timestamp on the diff separates them.

So linkage is fixable where infrastructure gives every record a shared id, and
not otherwise. An auditor building this method should link logs by such an id
wherever one exists. The call id here is safe only while the harness writes it
into both logs and the logs hold no duplicated or invented lines: an agent that
writes its own trace could forge lines, and \S\ref{sec:limit} shows what that does
(Table~\ref{tab:newloss}). Reusing a real action's call id to merge distinct
actions was not tested. All of this is post hoc, on the same episodes where the
problem was found.

\section{Loss the estimator does not expect}
\label{sec:limit}

Three loss patterns break the plain estimator under exact matching: loss that
depends on which action it is, loss that arrives in bursts, and a loss rate that
drifts over the episode. The second is partly repairable, at a width cost. None of
the estimator variants here repairs the first, although two cheaper checks survive
it (\S\ref{sec:cheap}). Duplicated and forged lines break it too, under the
linkages an auditor can compute. The action-dependent tables
below are under signature matching, the headline linkage; their third and fourth columns and
\texttt{confirm-mnar-hidden.json} show exact matching fails the same way. The
burst runs use exact matching except one row marked signature matching.

\paragraph{Loss that depends on the action.}

Every configuration so far deletes log lines independently of what the agent did.
The coupling experiment correlates the witnesses with each other; it never
correlates the loss with the actions. Real logging loss is not like that. Sampling
rules keyed to volume, bursts during load spikes and retries after the same crash
that produced an unusual action all concentrate loss on particular actions, and a
pooled capture-recapture model has no defence against that.

So I raised both logs' drop rate on exactly the operations the financial env
view cannot see: the actions every witness is then weakest on. This was built
after the confirmatory run and cannot change its verdicts. I first ran one dose,
1.8 times (Table~\ref{tab:mnar}). That turned out to be the dose at which the
pooled model looks least wrong, so Table~\ref{tab:mnardose} sweeps it.

\texttt{ll\_bic} fails silently from the smallest dose. At 1.1 times it covers
0.657 under signature matching and at 1.2 times 0.197 (0.647 and 0.187 under exact
matching), on intervals of ordinary width that still assert the gap in every
replicate (Figure~\ref{fig:dose}). It recovers at 1.6 and 1.8 times only because its
interval grows: at 1.8 times 0.11 of its intervals are unbounded and the finite
ones average 2161.1 wide against $N = 828$, which is not a measurement. Exact
matching gives the same curve, so this failure is the estimator's, not the
linkage's. I had predicted that latent classes would rescue the pooled model at
1.8 times. They did not: \texttt{lc\_gated} covers 0.910 but refuses in 0.78 of
replicates, and its finite intervals are biased by $-134.36$.

The claim therefore splits. \emph{Detection survives}: \texttt{ll\_bic} asserts
the logs are incomplete in nearly every replicate, and the union never does. That
is weak evidence on its own, since the independence model asserts it too in
every cell, including where it covers 0.000. \emph{Quantification does not}: when
loss concentrates on the actions no witness sees, a pooled model returns
intervals that look like counts and are wrong.

\paragraph{Conditioning on what the auditor can see.}
The loss above depends on the operation, and every captured record carries its
operation. That makes it missing-at-random given an observed covariate, which
is weaker than true MNAR. Within one operation, the logs lose lines by coin
flips at a constant rate. So I fitted the selected log-linear model per
operation, turned each profile likelihood into a density, and convolved the
strata (\texttt{strat\_bic}; the flat prior and the convolution are in
Appendix~\ref{app:methods}). A stratum with fewer than two live witnesses, or
none that disagree, contributes its observed count to the lower end and sends
the upper end to infinity.

Stratifying is calibrated where it answers (Table~\ref{tab:mnardose}). Up to
1.4 times its finite intervals cover between 0.953 and 0.979, while its refusal
rate climbs from 0.07 to 0.71 as the non-financial strata thin out, and they are
wider than \texttt{ll\_bic}'s. From 1.6 times it almost always refuses, so its
coverage there is refusal, which is \texttt{union\_bound} and says nothing. The
loss is keyed on the same variable it stratifies on, so this is close to its
best case. The stratified estimator also carries faults the decision log records
and leaves unfixed (D40): its flat prior is miscalibrated on thin strata, one
small stratum can make the whole total unbounded, and its tail extrapolation may
call a proper stratum unbounded. Some of its refusals may be those faults rather
than the data. A refusal is not a
diagnostic of action-dependent loss either: at the benign
\texttt{fin\_mid\_rho100}, where \texttt{ll\_bic} covers 0.930, \texttt{strat\_bic}
refuses in 1.00 of replicates, because coupled loss leaves the non-financial
strata with two identical witnesses.

\paragraph{A key no record carries.}
The boundary experiment moves the loss onto a key no record carries: every third
ledger id, with the env diff also blind to those actions. Stratifying on the
operation cannot see that key, and it should fail. Fifteen predictions were
committed before it and the stratified runs, and twelve held. Of the three that
did not, one missed by less than its Monte Carlo error. One I had not anticipated
at all: on thin strata a flat prior pushes the whole posterior up, so at
\texttt{fin\_sampled} every miss fell above $N$. The third put a ceiling on
coverage under coupled sampled loss that the estimator slightly exceeded.



Both fail (Table~\ref{tab:mnarstrat}, one seed of 100 replicates;
Table~\ref{tab:hiddenkey} reruns the same 1.8-times cell at 300 replicates and
finds both lower). \texttt{ll\_bic} covers 0.600.
\texttt{strat\_bic} covers 0.510 and refuses in 0.52 of replicates, and almost all
of that coverage is refusal: 50 of its 52 refusals covered, and 1 of its 48
finite intervals did. Separating the two parts (Table~\ref{tab:hiddenkey}) shows
the failure is mostly the env diff's blindness, not the extra log loss. With the
env diff blind to the keyed third and no extra loss, \texttt{ll\_bic} covers
0.160 and \texttt{strat\_bic} 0.467. With the env diff seeing them and the logs
losing them 1.8 times as often, 0.767 and 0.840. That is heterogeneity on an
unobserved key, the case the simulator's \texttt{l0\_heterogeneous} already showed
log-linear models cannot handle.

Neither estimator dominates. The pooled model assumes no three-way interaction
across strata, and the stratified model assumes independence inside each
two-witness stratum. The data cannot tell which is true. Where the stratified
estimator returns a finite interval and the pooled one disagrees, the stratified
one was the better calibrated at every dose tested here; where it refuses, it
says nothing,
and it refuses for thin strata whether or not the loss depends on the action.
Agreement between them proves nothing: they agree, and are both wrong, under
coupled hidden-key loss.

Most of that failure is not bursts as such. The agent works the queue one kind of
ticket at a time, so the actions the financial view cannot see sit in one stretch
of each episode: the env diff sees 0.095 of the first quarter of a
\texttt{long-60} episode and 0.223 of a \texttt{long-120} one, against 0.498 in

\paragraph{A dispersion correction.}
The standard tool for counts that vary more than Poisson is a quasi-likelihood
interval: estimate a dispersion $\hat\phi$ and scale the likelihood-ratio cutoff
by it~\citep{wedderburn1974quasi,mccullagh1989glm}. Where $\hat\phi$ comes from
decides whether it helps. Taken from the fit's own Pearson residuals it fails one
way or the other (Appendix~\ref{app:quasi}). With three witnesses the most
general model has no residual degrees of freedom, so the usual recipe of taking
it from a global model~\citep{lebreton1992marked,burnham2002model} has nothing to
work with. Replication across episodes does: under per-record loss each episode's
observed cells are a multinomial draw from the same proportions, and their
Pearson dispersion across episodes, $\hat\phi_{\mathrm{ep}}$, is near 1. It is a
homogeneity statistic and assumes exchangeable episodes, so it rises with any
difference between sessions, bursts included.

Two ways to use it were tried (Table~\ref{tab:intervalcomparison}). The first,
\texttt{ll\_qbic\_hom}, also selects the model on the quasi-likelihood scale, a
BIC analogue of QAIC~\citep{anderson1994overdispersed}; when session quality
varies and hits all three logs at once it drops the interactions that were
absorbing that dependence and collapses to 0.009. The second,
\texttt{ll\_bic\_hom}, keeps \texttt{ll\_bic}'s model and point estimate and only
scales the cutoff, so it can never cover less than \texttt{ll\_bic}. Under bursts
it raises coverage to 0.952 in back-office, 0.994 in infra and 0.985 under a joint
outage, at the plain width when loss is per-record and homogeneous. It does so by
widening around a point that may be biased: on long-60 it covers 0.894 at width
351.6 and detects the gap in 0.74 of replicates (0.877, 274.8 and 0.73 on the
loss seeds of Table~\ref{tab:controls}), and on long-120, which only that table has,
0.846 at 280.9 and 0.41. It is a width-for-coverage trade, not a repair, and the honest
report is the plain and corrected intervals side by side.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\input{tables/interval_comparison}
\caption{Three intervals against the losses that broke them. Post hoc.}
\label{tab:intervalcomparison}
\end{table}

Its cost is width. Any difference between sessions raises
$\hat\phi_{\mathrm{ep}}$: when capture rates vary by session, or back-office and
infra episodes are pooled, the plain interval is already nominal and
\texttt{ll\_bic\_hom} widens it by half to double. With little hidden, wide
intervals stop detecting the gap (0.006 in full-view bursts). It was not tested
on small audits; the QBIC variant kept coverage there but its gap detection fell
to 0.61 at eight episodes. The plain interval's burst cell spans 0.535 to 0.588 across loss-seed
sets (Table~\ref{tab:burstseeds}); \texttt{ll\_bic\_hom}'s is 0.952 here and 0.966 in
Table~\ref{tab:controls}. The correction was found after the runs,
on these episodes, and needs episode boundaries an auditor can see.

\paragraph{Whole-session outages and shared session quality.}
When the gateway or the trace is down for a whole session and the env diff stays
up, plain \texttt{ll\_bic} is nominal (0.936 and 0.943, Table~\ref{tab:outages})
and the correction costs four to seven times the width. That depends on the diff
staying up: when it can fail too, plain \texttt{ll\_bic} falls to between 0.235
and 0.379, and the correction recovers between 0.816 and 0.950 at intervals six
to eleven times wider. When the logs fail in a cascade, or all three together, no
interval here recovers anything. Session quality shared by all three logs, by
contrast, is not beyond reach, because episodes are observed: ranking them by how
much the logs saw and stratifying on six tiers (\texttt{tier\_bic}) gives 0.838
and 0.907 there, against 0.403 and 0.125 for \texttt{ll\_bic}
(Table~\ref{tab:sessionphase}). It is not a default. It covers less than
\texttt{ll\_bic} under per-record and coupled loss (0.932 and 0.912), does not
help bursts (0.595 and 0.770), collapses under action-dependent loss (0.292), and
its tier count is a free parameter (0.698 with four tiers).

\paragraph{A loss rate that drifts.}
Every loss model above holds the rate fixed within an episode. Here the trace's and
the gateway's drop rate runs linearly over the episode, from $1 - s$ to $1 + s$
times its mean, so the average loss is unchanged (Table~\ref{tab:newloss}). At
\texttt{fin\_mid}, under exact matching, \texttt{ll\_bic} covers 0.742 at slope
$s = 0.4$ and 0.471 at $s = 0.8$, with a point estimate biased upward by 9 and 13
actions; under signature matching it covers 0.443 and 0.318. The dispersion
correction was not run on these draws.

\paragraph{Faults in the lines themselves.}
Every loss above deletes lines. Logs also fail the other way: a retry writes a line
twice, a failed call is logged as a success, an agent writes lines for actions that
never happened. I applied these faults after the thinning, at \texttt{fin\_mid}
(Table~\ref{tab:newloss}). Exact matching reads the ledger id and ignores records
without one, so duplicates leave it at 0.946 and it is not an arm for phantoms; the
rows below are for the two linkages an auditor can compute. With 5\% duplicated
lines in all three logs \texttt{ll\_bic} covers 0.000 under signature matching (bias
+108) and 0.094 under call-id matching. Call-id matching removes duplicates in the
trace and the gateway, which share the id, and restores 0.946 when only they are
duplicated, but not those in the env diff, which has none. With 5\% forged
phantoms in all three logs, a line with its own call id and a resource no other
record has, it covers 0.000 under both linkages, and with 2\% it covers 0.042 and
0.063. Phantoms that copy a real record's signature do slightly less damage, 0.199
and 0.162 at 2\%. Forged lines in the trace alone, the one log an agent writes,
leave 0.169 and 0.218 at 5\% and 0.647 and 0.749 at 2\%. Reordering lines, by
swaps or by clock jitter, changed nothing in any arm.

\subsection{Cheaper checks}
\label{sec:cheap}

An auditor who runs the gateway, or who can plant actions, has two cheaper checks
than fitting a model; and Chapman's two-witness estimator needs only a pair of
logs. I ran all three on the confirmatory episodes with the same thinning,
500 replicates per cell, under exact matching unless noted (Table~\ref{tab:cheap}).
The cells are mostly the ones that broke \texttt{ll\_bic} above. All of it is post
hoc.

\emph{Sequence numbers.} The gateway numbers every action it routes, so its highest
number is $N$ when the last record survives. With half the records lost it does
not: the plain maximum covers 0.000 at \texttt{fin\_mid}, short by 20.89 on average.
A tail correction, which adds the expected number of records lost after the last one
that survived, covers 0.944 there at width 26.2 against \texttt{ll\_bic}'s 38.9.
It keeps covering where \texttt{ll\_bic} does not: 0.952 at 1.2 times the loss on
unseen operations, where \texttt{ll\_bic} covers 0.254 (0.187 in
Table~\ref{tab:mnardose}, whose 300 replicates use another set of loss seeds; the
two are about two standard errors apart), and 0.970 when every third action is
lost, against 0.690. It fails when its assumption does: its missing tail is
geometric, wrong when loss comes in runs or differs by session. Under bursts it
covers 0.000, short by 148.45. Under session quality
shared by all three logs it covers 0.480. And it counts only what the gateway
numbers: with 5\% of actions bypassing the gateway it covers 0.002, and with 20\%
0.000. Those two rows drop gateway records only; in the live bypass environment the
trace misses the bypassed actions too, so they say nothing about \texttt{ll\_bic}
under bypass. In scripted runs of that environment, in which 154 and 417 of 1474
actions are bypassed, the gateway's action count falls short of $N$ by exactly
those numbers (\texttt{bypass-scripted-10} and \texttt{-30}).

\emph{Canaries.} The auditor plants $k$ known actions per episode with the real
operation mix. They go through the same loss, and what the witnesses miss of them
gives the loss rates directly. With $k = 5$ the canary estimate covers at least
0.922 in every cell where canaries are exchangeable with the real actions, including
0.938 under bursts and 0.970 under shared session quality, where \texttt{ll\_bic}
covers 0.574 and 0.194. The price is width and gap detection: 106.0 wide against
38.9 per-record, and a gap detected in 0.69 of replicates against 1.0
(Table~\ref{tab:canary}). It needs enough canaries: gap detection is 0.000 at
$k = 1$ and 0.996 at $k = 10$. It fails if they are not exchangeable. Canaries
outside the loss rule's key cover 0.000. With a uniform operation mix instead of the
episode's, coverage at 1.8 times falls from 0.954 to 0.872. Three caveats flatter
it. A canary always has a record for the env diff when its operation is in the
diff's view, which real actions need not have. Canaries were never run with bypass.
And the battery ran on the confirmatory pool only.

\emph{Chapman.} The two-witness estimator covers 0.926 for the trace and the env
diff under fully coupled trace and gateway loss, at \texttt{ll\_bic}'s width (73.7
against 73.1), and 0.000 for the trace and the gateway, which are the coupled pair.
The trace--env pair covers 0.684 under bursts against \texttt{ll\_bic}'s 0.574, and
the trace--gateway pair 0.932 at 1.2 times loss on unseen operations, where
\texttt{ll\_bic} covers 0.254. But the first pair covers 0.162 and 0.000 under that
loss at 1.2 and 1.8 times, and the second covers 0.244 at 1.2 times under signature
matching and 0.314 even per-record. Which pair works depends on the loss and on the
linkage, and an auditor knows neither.

So none dominates. The counter wins where its numbering can be trusted and loss is
independent, at less width than \texttt{ll\_bic}. Canaries win on coverage wherever
they are exchangeable, with intervals wider than \texttt{ll\_bic}'s wherever those
are bounded and of ordinary size. \texttt{ll\_bic} needs neither a counter nor
planted actions, and fails silently in the cells where each of those works.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Integrity of agent records.} Hash chains and Merkle commitments detect
edits to one log~\citep{flightrecorder2026}. Trusted execution and notarised
transport make a trace host-independent~\citep{vet2025}, and systems audit frameworks
add tamper evidence against attackers who race the logger~\citep{paccagnella2020kenny,paccagnella2020custos}.
PeerReview has witnesses replay each other's tamper-evident logs to find omissions
deterministically~\citep{haeberlen2007peerreview}. All of these make a record
trustworthy; none estimates what is absent from it.

\paragraph{Monitoring in AI control.} Combining cost-constrained runtime monitors
improves detection under a budget~\citep{hua2025monitors}, and work on untrusted
monitors motivates collusion between a policy and its monitor, the adaptive
adversary of the simulator. Models claiming tool actions they never
took~\citep{transluce2025o3} is the reason self-report cannot be the only witness
and linkage has to tolerate fabricated records; no linkage here was tested
against them.

\paragraph{Capture-recapture beyond populations.} Software inspection used it to
estimate defects no reviewer found, and found inspector independence to be the
failure point~\citep{eick1992inspection,petersson2004inspection}. Fuzzing uses
species-richness estimators for residual risk~\citep{bohme2018stads}, and security
work used two-list estimates over phishing feeds~\citep{moore2007phish}. Over LLM
judges, it produced valid but uninformative intervals~\citep{kansal2026labels}.

\paragraph{Identification.} $N$ is not identified from the observed cells without
an assumption the data cannot test~\citep{aleshinguendel2024}; partial
identification gives confidence sets under bounded
dependence~\citep{sun2020bounds,dulcerubio2024}. The one-sided bounds here are an
instance of that family, anchored on the witness least controlled by the agent.

\section{What this does not show}
\label{sec:limits}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Benign agents only.} No live agent was given a reason to hide. Every
L1 and L2 number is from the parametric simulator, and nothing measured on benign
agents is evidence for the adversarial case that motivates the work.
\item \textbf{Simulated loss.} Logs were recorded complete and thinned afterwards:
before thinning the trace and the gateway each held every action. All hidden mass
is the thinning plus, in back-office, an env view cut to financial operations by
configuration; in infra the env diff's blindness comes from the domain. Coverage is over simulated faults, conditional on the recorded
behaviour, and every Monte Carlo error is over loss draws on a fixed pool of
episodes. Loss correlated with the actions breaks quantification, and loss in
bursts breaks the plain interval; a dispersion correction trades width for
coverage and does not remove the bias (\S\ref{sec:limit}).
\item \textbf{Single seeds in the exploratory sections.} The live exploratory
tables and the 1.8-times MNAR tables are one loss seed of 100 replicates each.
The confirmatory precision, the MNAR dose response and the false-gap battery
replace some of them; the rest are reported as single draws.
\item \textbf{One model family, two environments and a batch-tool variant of one, all
designed by me.} Two versions of one model. The second environment tests the environment, the loss model and the
linkage, not the agent: the paid run reproduces a scripted one, and its verdict
depends on a collision density its ticket mix sets (\S\ref{sec:transfer}).
The third tests a stipulated channel: no witness parses a script, and the share
of actions bypassed (0.856) is the agent's choice under a parser that rejected 34 of 99
scripts, not a setting I controlled.
\item \textbf{The bypass result is one pool and one design, at the boundary of the
method's scope.} It is 24 episodes of one model, with a no-bypass reference from a
different pool, and a fail reads as an effect of bypass by a registered rule rather than
a controlled comparison. Under the registered view 96\% of the hidden mass is bypassed
customer updates that no witness records, so the registered cell asks for extrapolation
to actions with zero capture probability; parsing the scripts is a post hoc change to the
logs. The audit-warning prompt is one pilot of 12 episodes and one fixed prompt, and the
share difference rests on two neutral episodes; it is not an adversarial agent, and an
agent that tried to defeat the audit could do worse. No share threshold is established.
\item \textbf{Exploration before registration, analysis after.} The
pre-registrations cover the three confirmatory arms only. Everything before them,
including the estimator choice, was seen first, and everything in
\S\ref{sec:linkage} and \S\ref{sec:limit} came after, including the linkage
arms and the dispersion-corrected intervals.
\item \textbf{All three registered coverage tests missed, in expectation.} The
back-office test passed on its registered seed and covers 0.893 at 3000
replicates; the transfer test failed outright, and so did the bypass test (0.759). 21 of the 24 planned back-office
episodes completed, and which were lost depended on the trajectory, not the seed.
The crash rerun and the restoration both deviate from the protocol.
\item \textbf{A numbered log can be enough, in some settings.} Every action in
the back-office environment passes the gateway. A gateway that numbered its action
entries and logged its counter would give $N$ exactly there. With record loss and
no logged counter, a tail-corrected sequence number works under independent loss,
including loss concentrated on unseen operations, and fails under bursts, under
session quality shared by all three logs and with any bypass (\S\ref{sec:cheap}).
The method's case rests on loss that varies in time or by session, and on logs that
are not numbered. The simulator models both; the live loss runs do not. The one live
bypass study (\S\ref{sec:bypass}) is the boundary, a channel that reaches no counted log:
the counter was at 0.0, and \texttt{ll\_bic} could not bound $N$ there.
\item \textbf{The cheaper checks ran in simulation only, on one pool.} The
canary estimate gives every canary a record for the env diff whenever its operation
is in the diff's view, was never run with bypass, and ran on the confirmatory
episodes only. The bypass rows for the sequence number drop gateway records only;
in the live bypass environment the trace misses those actions too, and there the
counter covered 0.0 (\S\ref{sec:bypass}). Canaries were not run with bypass.
\item \textbf{Linkage by shared id is only as good as the id.} The tool-call
id that restores back-office coverage under the financial view is written into
both logs by the harness. Forged phantom lines and duplicated lines break it
(\S\ref{sec:limit}); reusing a real action's id to merge distinct actions was not
tested. Linking the environment diff, which carries no id, remains open
(\S\ref{sec:linkage}).
\item \textbf{The dispersion correction rests on exchangeable episodes.} Its
dispersion is a homogeneity statistic, so any difference between sessions widens
the interval. It was tested on aligned blocks, whole-session outages and
session-level rates, on pools of five to twenty-four episodes, and not on small
audits.
\item \textbf{Forged lines were simulated, not observed.} Duplicated, cloned and
forged lines were injected into recorded logs at fixed rates, in all three logs and
in the trace alone, and they break \texttt{ll\_bic} (\S\ref{sec:limit}). No agent
was asked to write them, and an adversary that shapes them to keep the capture
table consistent was not tried.
\item \textbf{The agent can read its own trace.} W1's file is read-only to the
agent, and the agent read it in most confirmatory episodes. With loss injected
afterwards this cannot touch the independence the estimators assume. Against an
agent with a reason to hide, it is a channel that study would have to close.
\end{itemize}

\section{Reproducing this}
\label{sec:reproduce}

The repository, \url{https://github.com/caiotheodoro/lacuna}, holds the
environment, the estimators, every recorded episode and every artifact a number
in this paper comes from. The capture histories are also a dataset at
\url{https://huggingface.co/datasets/caiotheodoro/lacuna-capture-histories},
without the episode transcripts. \texttt{make validate} runs the
tests, including one that fails if a figure in the README is absent from the
measurement card, and \texttt{scripts/paper\_tables.py} regenerates every table here
from the artifacts. The simulator sweep is \texttt{make sim}, and the confirmatory
analysis is \texttt{scripts/thin\_episodes.py -{}-run confirm -{}-pool heldout
-{}-bootstrap-episodes} followed by \texttt{scripts/test\_hypotheses.py}. The flag
resamples the recorded episodes with replacement in each replicate, as registered.
Without it \texttt{ll\_bic} at \texttt{fin\_mid} under signature matching covers
0.90 rather than 0.94 (\texttt{artifacts/thinning/confirm-heldout-fixed-episodes.json}).
At 100 replicates each, with a Monte Carlo error of about 0.03, the two are not
distinguishable, and the fixed-episode run still reaches the 0.90 bar.
\texttt{make repro-headline} reruns the registered analysis into a temporary
directory: H1--H4 and every \texttt{ll\_bic}, \texttt{ll\_independence} and union
field reproduce exactly. \texttt{ll\_average} and \texttt{strat\_lc\_gated} at
\texttt{fin\_mid\_rho100} differ slightly (8 fields; the solver sits on boundary fits,
and a library version since the run is the likely cause); no number in this paper
depends on them. The post-hoc robustness checks are
\texttt{scripts/precision.py}, \texttt{mnar\_dose.py}, \texttt{falsegap\_battery.py},
\texttt{collisions.py}, \texttt{linkage\_arms.py}, \texttt{burst\_loss.py},
\texttt{overdispersion.py}, \texttt{episode\_dispersion.py},
\texttt{interval\_comparison.py}, \texttt{session\_and\_phase.py} and
\texttt{op\_position.py}, \texttt{baselines\_battery.py},
\texttt{new\_loss\_battery.py} and \texttt{selection\_bootstrap.py}, for the
second environment \texttt{scripts/transfer\_robustness.py}, and for the third
\texttt{scripts/bypass\_analysis.py} and \texttt{scripts/bypass\_verdicts.py}, which
are frozen by hash in the registration, and the post hoc
\texttt{scripts/bypass\_counterfactuals.py}; none calls a model.
\texttt{scripts/r\_crosscheck.py} runs R in a pinned Docker image. All live runs together cost
\$7.30, of which \$0.29 was diagnostic reruns; the bypass study's two runs were
\$1.37 of that, and the first, crashed confirmatory attempt has no receipt. The decision log, \texttt{docs/DECISIONS.md}, has a dated entry for every prediction
written before a run and every result that contradicted one.

\paragraph{Loss.} Each witness record is dropped with its log's probability
(Table~\ref{tab:configs}), independently unless the configuration couples it.
Under $\rho$ coupling, an action is coupled with probability $\rho$; a coupled
action draws one uniform $u$ that its gateway and trace records share, and each
record is dropped when $u$ is below its log's drop rate. Since the gateway drops
at least as often as the trace, a coupled action that lost its trace line lost
its gateway line too. Under exclusive loss the trace record uses $1-u$, so with
drop rates summing to at most 1 the two logs never drop the same action. The
\texttt{financial} env view keeps W3 records only for refunds, cancellations and
outbound mail. MNAR configurations multiply both logs' drop rate, capped at 1,
for the named operations; the hidden-key ones do it for every third ledger id
and also remove those actions from W3.

\paragraph{W3.} The env witness is computed from the kernel's state, not from
the ledger: a diff of its tables between the start and the end of the episode,
plus the outbound mail sink. A change made and reverted inside an episode leaves
no diff row, and repeated changes to one field show as one. A ledger id is
attached to W3 rows only for oracle linkage.

\paragraph{Signature linkage.} A signature is the operation, the resource and,
for some operations, the value: in back-office refunds, customer updates,
cancellations and file deletions; in infra scaling, deploys, rollbacks, config
updates and incidents. File, email, page and runbook bodies are left out, because
an LLM auditor reading a transcript cannot reproduce a hash of text it
paraphrased; \S\ref{sec:linkage} shows the cost of that choice. Within one signature, if witness
$w$ has $c_w$ records there are $\max_w c_w$ actions, and records are paired in
order: the $j$-th action is seen by every witness with more than $j$ records.
Agent-supplied identifiers are never used.

\paragraph{Scoring.} The coverage target is the ledger count of the
replicate's resampled episodes, under both linkages. An interval $[lo, hi]$
covers when $lo \le N \le hi$, so an unbounded interval covers whenever its lower
end is at most $N$; tables with a ``refuses'' column or superscript report how
often that happens. Gap detection is
$lo$ above the number of distinct actions the three witnesses saw, after linkage.
Mean widths are over bounded intervals and bias over finite point estimates.
The log-linear intervals are also reported unbounded when the fit does not
converge or its point estimate is not finite, and \texttt{lc} when its upper end
passes 20 times the observed count.

\paragraph{Log-linear models.} The candidates are every subset of the three
pairwise interactions, eight models, with no three-way term. Each is a Poisson
GLM on the seven observed cells, fitted by iteratively reweighted least squares.
The interval is a 95\% profile likelihood on the full multinomial, searched up
to 20 times the observed count; a profile that does not close below that is
reported unbounded. \texttt{ll\_bic}'s interval is conditional on the selected
model. \texttt{ll\_average} instead mixes the models' normalised profile
likelihoods with BIC weights, with the mass past the search cap as one atom at
infinity; models below $10^{-3}$ of the best weight are dropped.

\paragraph{Checks on the log-linear fits.} I refitted 28 capture tables against R's
\texttt{Rcapture} (224 fits; \texttt{r-crosscheck.json}). Point estimates agree to
$1.8 \times 10^{-9}$ relative and the BIC choice agrees in all 28. The interval is
the exact profile of the Cormack likelihood and matches an independent exact
computation in R to $4 \times 10^{-7}$; \texttt{Rcapture}'s own printed endpoints
differ from both by up to 7.2 units, because of a Stirling approximation. 33 of the
224 fits are boundary cases, listed in the artifact: on the unthinned and
\texttt{fin\_mid\_rho100} confirmatory tables our fit is unbounded or unconverged
where R's is finite. A nested parametric bootstrap that redoes the BIC selection in
every resample, to price the selection the profile interval ignores, is a null at
\texttt{fin\_mid}: coverage moves by $+0.005$ (paired Monte Carlo error 0.006). In
the simulator it lowers gap detection, from 0.3225 to 0.105 at
\texttt{l0\_independent} and from 0.9325 to 0.735 at \texttt{l1\_hides\_from\_trace},
and it does not repair \texttt{l0\_heterogeneous}, where it covers 0.3325
(\texttt{selection-coverage.json}). Neither check changes a number above.

\paragraph{Dispersion-corrected intervals.} With per-episode capture tables
$y_{ec}$ over the seven observed cells $c$, episode sizes $n_e$ and pooled
proportions $\hat p_c$, the between-episode dispersion is
$\hat\phi_{\mathrm{ep}} = \max\{1, X^2/[(E-1)(C-1)]\}$ with
$X^2 = \sum_{e,c} (y_{ec} - n_e\hat p_c)^2 / (n_e \hat p_c)$, over the $E$ non-empty
episodes and $C$ cells seen at all, $n_e = \sum_c y_{ec}$, and $\hat\phi_{\mathrm{ep}}
= 1$ when $E < 2$: the Pearson statistic for homogeneity of the episodes, analogous
to the corrections of chi-squared tests under cluster
sampling~\citep{brier1980cluster,rao1981complex}. \texttt{ll\_bic\_hom} fits
\texttt{ll\_bic}'s model to the pooled table and cuts its profile likelihood at
$\hat\phi_{\mathrm{ep}}\,\chi^2_{1,0.95}/2$ below the maximum.
\texttt{ll\_qbic\_hom} instead selects the model minimising $-2\log L /
\hat\phi_{\mathrm{ep}} + p \log n$, with $p$ the number of parameters and $n$ the
observed count, and uses the same cutoff.

\paragraph{Latent classes.} \texttt{lc} is a latent-class model with a
truncated stick-breaking prior over at most 10 classes, concentration
$\alpha \sim \mathrm{Gamma}(0.25, 0.25)$, uniform priors on capture
probabilities and a $1/N$ prior on $N$, sampled by Gibbs on the cell counts with
1000 burn-in and 2000 kept draws. Its interval is the central 95\% of the
posterior. \texttt{lc\_gated} reports $[n, \infty)$ instead when any converged
log-linear model within 6 BIC of the best is unbounded, or has a point estimate
outside \texttt{lc}'s interval.

\paragraph{Stratified.} \texttt{strat\_bic} fits the BIC-selected log-linear
model per operation, after dropping witnesses that saw nothing in that stratum.
A stratum is identified only with at least two live witnesses that disagree
somewhere. Its profile likelihood over $N_s \ge n_s$ is normalised into a
density, which is a flat prior on $N_s$, with a power-law tail past the search
cap. The densities are convolved and the total's 2.5, 50 and 97.5\% points
reported. An unidentified stratum, or one whose density cannot be normalised,
adds its observed count to the lower end and sends the upper end to infinity.
